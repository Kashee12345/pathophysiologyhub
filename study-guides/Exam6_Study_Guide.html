TOPIC 1 🥬 Kidney Function, Labs & UTI MUST KNOW▼

The kidney filters blood ~50 times a day and controls fluid, electrolytes, and pH. Start with the labs that measure it and the infections that attack it.

Kidney function tests

TestWhat it means
GFRBlood filtered per minute — the key measure of kidney function. GFR falls as disease worsens (end-stage < 15).
CreatinineMuscle-breakdown waste cleared by the kidney. As creatinine ↑, GFR ↓ — used to stage kidney disease.
BUNBlood urea nitrogen (protein-breakdown waste); rises with declining function and dehydration.

Urinary tract infection: lower vs upper

Lower UTIUpper UTI
Bladder/urethra — example: acute cystitis. Urgency, frequency, dysuria, suprapubic discomfort.Kidney — example: acute pyelonephritis. Flank pain, fever, chills, systemic illness. Usually ascending from a lower UTI.
🧠 Memory hooks
Creatinine UP = GFR DOWN (they seesaw). Lower UTI = cystitis (burning/frequency). Upper UTI = pyelonephritis (flank pain + fever).
⚠ Common confusion
A rising creatinine signals a falling GFR, not better kidneys. A lower UTI that ascends becomes pyelonephritis with fever and flank pain.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. A patient's creatinine is rising. What is happening to GFR, and why?
    Show answer
    GFR is falling. Creatinine is cleared by filtration, so when filtration drops it accumulates — the two move in opposite directions.
  2. A patient has flank pain, fever, and chills. Lower or upper UTI, and which condition?
    Show answer
    Upper UTI → acute pyelonephritis. Flank pain + fever distinguish it from lower-tract cystitis.
  3. SATA: Which are features of cystitis? (a) frequency, (b) dysuria, (c) high fever with flank pain, (d) urgency, (e) suprapubic discomfort.
    Show answer
    a, b, d, e. (c) points to upper-tract pyelonephritis.
🩺 Clinical scenario
A woman treated for a bladder infection now has a 102°F fever, chills, and right flank pain. What happened?

Her lower UTI ascended to the kidney → acute pyelonephritis. New fever and flank pain signal kidney involvement needing prompt systemic treatment.
TOPIC 2 💧 Nephrotic vs Nephritic Syndrome MUST KNOW▼

Two glomerular syndromes students mix up every term. The difference is what leaks out of the glomerulus.

Nephrotic syndromeNephritic syndrome (AGN)
PathoMembrane leaky selectively to PROTEINInflammation (immune complexes) → leaky to everything (RBCs, WBCs, protein)
Main causesDiabetes mellitus, lupusUntreated strep (post-streptococcal GN)
HallmarksProteinuria >3.5 g/day (foamy urine), hypoalbuminemia, edema (periorbital/extremities), hyperlipidemiaHematuria ("cola-colored" urine), RBC/WBC in urine, hypertension (Na/fluid retention)
🧠 Memory hooks
NephrOtic = prOtein & edema (foamy urine, low albumin; diabetes/lupus). NephrItic = Inflammation & hematuria (cola urine + hypertension; post-strep).
⚠ Common confusion
Nephrotic leaks protein (edema); nephritic is inflammatory and leaks blood (cola urine, HTN). Only nephritic is the classic post-strep picture.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. A patient has proteinuria >3.5 g/day, foamy urine, low albumin, and periorbital edema. Which syndrome?
    Show answer
    Nephrotic syndrome. The glomerulus is selectively leaky to protein → heavy proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, edema, hyperlipidemia (diabetes/lupus).
  2. A child has cola-colored urine and hypertension two weeks after untreated strep throat. Which syndrome and mechanism?
    Show answer
    Nephritic syndrome (acute glomerulonephritis). Post-strep antigen–antibody complexes inflame the glomerulus → hematuria and Na/fluid retention (HTN).
  3. Which finding best distinguishes nephritic from nephrotic syndrome?
    Show answer
    Hematuria with inflammation = nephritic; heavy proteinuria with edema = nephrotic.
🩺 Clinical scenario
A long-standing diabetic develops leg/facial swelling, foamy urine, and high cholesterol. Which syndrome and why?

Nephrotic syndrome — chronic diabetes damaged the glomerular membrane, leaking protein → hypoalbuminemia (edema), foamy urine, and compensatory hyperlipidemia.
TOPIC 3 ⚡ Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) MUST KNOW▼

Acute kidney injury (AKI) = sudden decline in kidney function (hours–days), often reversible if caught early. Classify by where the problem is.

TypeProblemCauses
Pre-renal↓ blood flow TO the kidney (nephrons initially healthy)Hypovolemia (dehydration, hemorrhage, shock), heart failure, atherosclerosis
Intra-renalDamage INSIDE the kidneyAcute tubular necrosis, glomerulonephritis, nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, IV contrast, vancomycin)
Post-renalObstruction AFTER the kidney → back-pressureBPH, tumor, stones → hydroureter/hydronephrosis

Acute tubular necrosis (ATN)

The most common intra-renal cause: tubular cells die (ischemia or toxins) → slough off → tubular obstruction → oliguria and edema.

Phases of recovery

PhaseWhat happens
1. Oliguric<400 mL/24 hr; ↑ BUN/creatinine, fatigue, fluid-overload risk
2. Diuretic2–4 L/day; risk of dehydration, hypovolemia, hypotension; BUN/Cr stabilize
3. RecoveryUrine normalizes (1–2 L/day); GFR improves; BUN/Cr decrease
🧠 Memory hooks
Pre = flow problem, Intra = internal damage, Post = plumbing/obstruction. Recovery: Oliguric → Diuretic → Recovery.
⚠ Common confusion
The diuretic phase is deceptive — the big urine output isn't "all better"; tubules can't concentrate yet, so watch for dehydration and electrolyte loss.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. A patient in hypovolemic shock develops AKI. Which classification, and why are nephrons initially undamaged?
    Show answer
    Pre-renal AKI. Reduced blood flow to the kidney — nephrons are fine but can't filter what they don't receive. Restoring perfusion often reverses it.
  2. SATA: Which cause intra-renal AKI? (a) acute tubular necrosis, (b) NSAIDs/IV contrast, (c) BPH obstruction, (d) glomerulonephritis, (e) dehydration.
    Show answer
    a, b, d. BPH is post-renal; dehydration is pre-renal.
  3. In which AKI phase is the patient at highest risk for dehydration and hypotension?
    Show answer
    The diuretic phase — output surges (2–4 L/day) but the kidney can't concentrate it yet.
🩺 Clinical scenario
An older man can't urinate, has a distended bladder, rising creatinine, and an enlarged prostate. Which AKI type?

Post-renal AKI from BPH obstructing outflow → urine backs up (hydroureter → hydronephrosis), pressure rises, GFR falls. Relieving the obstruction is the fix.
TOPIC 4 ⌛ Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) MUST KNOW▼

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) = permanent, progressive nephron loss → declining GFR → fluid, waste, and electrolyte buildup. It's a multisystem disorder.

Most common causes: diabetes and hypertension.

Increased (↑)Decreased (↓)
Creatinine & BUN, potassium, hydrogen ions (acidosis), phosphateGFR, calcium, vitamin D, erythropoietin (→ anemia)

Why those labs

  • ↓ EPO → anemia (fatigue, pallor).
  • ↑ potassium (hyperkalemia) — can't excrete it → dangerous for the heart.
  • ↑ H⁺ → metabolic acidosis.
  • ↓ vitamin D activation → ↓ calcium → bone disease; phosphate rises.
  • Fluid retention → edema, hypertension.

Management: low sodium/potassium diet, avoid magnesium-containing laxatives, vitamin D + calcium supplements; end-stage (GFR < 15) → dialysis or transplant.

🧠 Memory hooks
CKD's top causes = Diabetes & Hypertension. Labs: everything up EXCEPT calcium, GFR, vitamin D, and EPO. ↓EPO → anemia; can't dump K⁺ → hyperkalemia.
⚠ Common confusion
In CKD calcium goes DOWN (impaired vitamin D activation) while phosphate goes up — don't lump calcium with the "everything is high" group.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. What are the two most common causes of CKD?
    Show answer
    Diabetes and hypertension — both damage the kidney's small vessels over time.
  2. A CKD patient has fatigue and pallor. Which lab change explains it?
    Show answer
    Decreased erythropoietin → fewer RBCs → anemia.
  3. SATA: In CKD, which labs DECREASE? (a) GFR, (b) calcium, (c) potassium, (d) vitamin D, (e) erythropoietin.
    Show answer
    a, b, d, e. Potassium rises (can't be excreted).
🩺 Clinical scenario
A patient with 20 years of poorly controlled diabetes and hypertension has GFR 12, high potassium, low calcium, and anemia. What stage, and what's next?

End-stage CKD. Nephron loss dropped GFR below 15, causing hyperkalemia, low calcium (low vitamin D), and anemia (low EPO). Typically requires dialysis or transplant.
TOPIC 5 🪨 Stones, PKD, Bladder Cancer & Incontinence HIGH YIELD▼

Common urologic disorders that show up in practice and on the exam.

DisorderKey points
Nephrolithiasis (kidney stones)Concentrated minerals crystallize (most often calcium). Risk: hypercalcemia (hyperparathyroidism), high uric acid, low fluid intake. Abrupt severe flank pain radiating to the groin, vomiting, ± hematuria — pain begins when the stone moves from kidney into ureter. Prevention: hydration.
Polycystic kidney diseaseInherited — multiple cysts enlarge kidneys and destroy tissue. Often asymptomatic early; then flank pain, hematuria, nocturia, hypertension, ± palpable kidneys.
Bladder cancerMost commonly urothelial carcinoma. Classic sign: painless hematuria; may mimic UTI; risk with smoking/chemicals.
Urinary incontinenceOverflow = retention/obstruction (BPH; males > females; infection risk). Stress = sphincter weakness (leak with cough/laugh; females, pregnancies, age). Urge/OAB = overactive detrusor (sudden strong urges).
🧠 Memory hooks
Stones = colicky flank-to-groin pain (hydrate to prevent). PKD = inherited cysts → big kidneys + HTN. Bladder cancer = PAINLESS hematuria. Incontinence: Overflow / Stress / Urge.
⚠ Common confusion
Hematuria clues: stones = painful; bladder cancer = painless; nephritic syndrome = cola-colored with inflammation.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. A patient has sudden severe flank pain radiating to the groin with vomiting. Which disorder, and when does pain start?
    Show answer
    Nephrolithiasis. Pain begins as the stone passes from kidney into the ureter; hydration prevents stones by keeping urine dilute.
  2. A patient reports painless blood in the urine and a smoking history. What must be ruled out?
    Show answer
    Bladder cancer. Painless hematuria (often urothelial carcinoma) is the classic presentation, especially with smoking.
  3. A man with BPH has chronic bladder distension and dribbling. Which incontinence type?
    Show answer
    Overflow incontinence — obstruction/retention makes the overfull bladder leak; stasis also raises infection risk.
🩺 Clinical scenario
A 35-year-old with a family history of kidney disease has hypertension, flank discomfort, nocturia, and bilaterally enlarged lumpy kidneys. Diagnosis?

Polycystic kidney disease — inherited cysts enlarge the kidneys and replace functional tissue, producing hypertension, hematuria/nocturia, and palpable kidneys.
TOPIC 6 💨 Gas Exchange, V/Q & Lung Sounds MUST KNOW▼

Respiration is a three-step sequence. Every lung disease breaks one of these steps — name the broken step and the symptoms follow.

ComponentWhat it isDiseases that break it
VentilationMoving air in/out of alveoli (obstructive problems)Asthma, COPD, OSA, cystic fibrosis, bronchitis
DiffusionO₂/CO₂ crossing the alveolar–capillary membranePulmonary edema, pneumonia, ARDS, emphysema
PerfusionBlood flow to the alveoliPulmonary embolism, pulmonary hypertension

V/Q mismatch

  • Shunt = perfusion to an area that isn't ventilated (blood but no air) → COPD, mucus plugging, pneumonia.
  • Dead space = ventilation to an area that isn't perfused (air but no blood) → classic in pulmonary embolism.

Hypoxia vs hypoxemia, and lung sounds

  • Hypoxemia = low oxygen in the blood; hypoxia = low oxygen in the tissues. Signs: tachycardia, cyanosis, restlessness, dyspnea.
  • Crackles (rales) = air through fluid. Wheezes = narrowed airways. Rhonchi = mucus in large airways (snoring). Stridor = upper-airway narrowing.
🧠 Memory hooks
Shunt = blood, no air; Dead space = air, no blood (PE). Crackles = fluid, Wheeze = tight airway, Rhonchi = mucus.
⚠ Common confusion
Hypoxemia (blood) precedes hypoxia (tissues). A PE is a perfusion problem — ventilation is fine, blood can't reach that region.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. A PE blocks blood flow to a ventilated lung region. Which V/Q abnormality?
    Show answer
    Dead space — ventilation without perfusion. PE is fundamentally a perfusion problem.
  2. You hear crackles. What do they indicate?
    Show answer
    Fluid in the alveoli (pneumonia, pulmonary edema, heart failure). Wheezes = tight airways; rhonchi = mucus.
  3. Difference between hypoxemia and hypoxia?
    Show answer
    Hypoxemia = low oxygen in the blood; hypoxia = low oxygen in the tissues.
🩺 Clinical scenario
A patient breathes rapidly but oxygen stays low; a large clot sits in a pulmonary artery. Why is oxygen low despite good breathing?

The clot creates dead space — that region is ventilated but not perfused, so no gas exchange occurs. The broken step is perfusion.
TOPIC 7 🌬 COPD: Chronic Bronchitis vs Emphysema MUST KNOW▼

COPD is progressive, not-fully-reversible airflow obstruction — an umbrella for chronic bronchitis and emphysema (often both). The core problem is getting air out.

Chronic bronchitisEmphysema
PathoHypersecretion of mucus + airway inflammation from a chronic irritant/toxin (not infection)Destruction of alveoli + loss of surfactant/elastic recoil → can't exhale fully (air trapping)
SignsChronic productive cough, dyspnea, hypoxemia, hypercapniaBarrel chest, prolonged expiration, dyspnea, pursed-lip breathing

The hypoxic drive

In chronic COPD, CO₂ is always high, so the body relies on low oxygen to stimulate breathing (hypoxic drive). Too much oxygen can slow their breathing, letting CO₂ climb dangerously — titrate O₂ to a target rather than high-flow.

🧠 Memory hooks
Chronic Bronchitis = mucus & cough (hypercapnia). Emphysema = barrel chest & air trapping. COPD = "getting air out is the problem." Too much O₂ suppresses the hypoxic drive.
⚠ Common confusion
Chronic bronchitis is from an irritant/toxin (smoking), not infection. COPD obstruction is about exhalation, unlike restrictive disease (trouble inhaling).

❓ Practice Questions

  1. A long-time smoker has a barrel chest, prolonged expiration, and destroyed alveoli. Which COPD component?
    Show answer
    Emphysema — alveolar destruction with loss of recoil traps air.
  2. Why give oxygen cautiously in chronic COPD?
    Show answer
    They rely on a hypoxic drive; too much oxygen slows respirations and lets CO₂ build up. Titrate to a target SpO₂.
  3. What causes chronic bronchitis, and its hallmark signs?
    Show answer
    A chronic irritant/toxin (smoking), not infection → mucus hypersecretion → chronic productive cough, dyspnea, hypoxemia, hypercapnia.
🩺 Clinical scenario
A COPD patient with SpO₂ 86% is placed on high-flow oxygen and becomes drowsy with slowed breathing. What happened?

Excess oxygen blunted the hypoxic drive, so respirations slowed and CO₂ rose (CO₂ narcosis → drowsiness). Oxygen should be titrated to a controlled target.
TOPIC 8 🫁 Asthma & Other Chronic Lung Disease MUST KNOW▼

The other chronic lung diseases: asthma (reversible), plus occupational, sleep, vascular, and genetic conditions.

DiseasePatho & signs
AsthmaTwo problems: bronchoconstriction + inflammation (mucus, swelling). Reversible. S/S: dyspnea/chest tightness, wheezing, cough (worse at night); triggers: exercise, allergens
Cystic fibrosisAutosomal recessive → abnormally thick mucus → recurrent lung infections, hypoxia, poor growth
Pneumoconiosis ("black lung")Inhaled dust (coal, silica, asbestos) → scarring → obstruction and poor lung expansion
Obstructive sleep apneaThroat muscles relax and block the airway in sleep. S/S: daytime fatigue, memory issues; risk: obesity, male, alcohol. Can cause pulmonary HTN → right HF
Pulmonary hypertensionHigh pulmonary artery pressure → right ventricular strain → right-sided heart failure. Secondary causes: COPD, PE, OSA, fibrosis
🧠 Memory hooks
Asthma = bronchoconstriction + inflammation (reversible). CF = autosomal recessive, thick mucus. Pneumoconiosis = dust. OSA/chronic lung disease → pulmonary HTN → right heart failure.
⚠ Common confusion
Asthma vs COPD: asthma is reversible and trigger-driven; COPD is chronic, not fully reversible (usually smoking).

❓ Practice Questions

  1. What are the two core mechanisms of an asthma attack?
    Show answer
    Bronchoconstriction plus inflammation (mucus and swelling) → obstruction, wheezing, cough.
  2. Cystic fibrosis inheritance pattern and core lung problem?
    Show answer
    Autosomal recessive; abnormally thick mucus → recurrent infections, hypoxia, poor growth.
  3. How does OSA lead to right-sided heart failure?
    Show answer
    Repeated nighttime hypoxia raises pulmonary pressure → pulmonary hypertension → right-ventricular strain → right-sided heart failure.
🩺 Clinical scenario
A retired coal miner has progressive dyspnea with lung scarring; a young patient has episodic wheezing and night cough triggered by cats. Name each.

The miner has pneumoconiosis from coal dust → fibrosis. The younger patient has asthma — reversible bronchoconstriction and inflammation from an allergen.
TOPIC 9 🦠 Acute Infections: Pneumonia, Flu & COVID MUST KNOW▼

The acute respiratory infections — from a mild chest cold to lobar pneumonia and the two big viruses.

Upper respiratory infections (usually viral, self-limiting)

Pharyngitis (sore throat), sinusitis (facial pain/congestion), rhinitis (runny nose), laryngitis (hoarse voice). Treatment is usually supportive care.

ConditionPatho & signs
Acute bronchitisAcute, usually viral ("chest cold"); inflamed bronchi, cough, often rhonchi; self-limiting — usually no antibiotics
PneumoniaAcute, usually bacterial, localized to one lobe; alveoli fill with fluid/exudate. S/S: fever, productive cough, crackles, hypoxia, dyspnea, confusion (elderly)
InfluenzaVirus attacks respiratory epithelial cells. S/S: high fever, body aches, headache, dry cough, sore throat
COVID-19Targets alveolar epithelial cells (type II pneumocytes) via the ACE-2 receptor → deep lower-respiratory inflammation and rapid oxygen decline
🧠 Memory hooks
Acute bronchitis = viral chest cold (rhonchi, no antibiotics). Pneumonia = bacterial, localized, fluid-filled alveoli. Flu = upper epithelium; COVID = alveoli via ACE-2.
⚠ Common confusion
Acute vs chronic bronchitis: acute = viral, self-limiting; chronic = long-term irritant (smoking), part of COPD.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. A patient has fever, productive cough, crackles in one lobe, and hypoxia. Which infection, and what fills the alveoli?
    Show answer
    Pneumonia (usually bacterial, localized). Alveoli fill with fluid/inflammatory exudate, impairing gas exchange.
  2. Why does COVID drop oxygen more than influenza?
    Show answer
    COVID targets alveolar cells via the ACE-2 receptor — right where gas exchange happens. Flu mainly affects upper-airway epithelium.
  3. Should acute viral bronchitis get antibiotics?
    Show answer
    No. It's usually viral and self-limiting — supportive care is the treatment.
🩺 Clinical scenario
An 80-year-old is brought in confused with fever, wet cough, crackles over the right lower lobe, and SpO₂ 88%. Diagnosis?

Pneumonia — a localized alveolar infection filling air sacs with fluid. In the elderly, new confusion is often an early sign.
TOPIC 10 🚨 Emergencies: ARDS, PE & Atelectasis MUST KNOW▼

The acute lung emergencies — fast onset, fast deterioration.

ConditionPatho & signs
ARDSMassive pulmonary inflammation → leaky capillaries → alveolar edema. Triggers: SEPSIS, burns, trauma. S/S: severe dyspnea, rapid breathing, refractory hypoxemia
Pulmonary embolismA dislodged thrombus (usually leg DVT) blocks a pulmonary artery → dead space. Risk: DVT, immobility, femur fracture, birth control, smoking, obesity. S/S: sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, hemoptysis, hypoxemia
AtelectasisCollapse of alveoli (bronchiole blockage or post-op shallow breathing) → that area can't exchange gas
🧠 Memory hooks
ARDS = body-wide inflammatory hit (sepsis/burns/trauma) → leaky alveoli. PE = sudden dyspnea + pleuritic pain + hemoptysis. Atelectasis = collapsed alveoli (post-op — incentive spirometry).
⚠ Common confusion
ARDS vs pneumonia: pneumonia is a localized infection; ARDS is diffuse whole-lung inflammation from a systemic trigger with refractory hypoxemia.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. A septic patient develops severe dyspnea and hypoxemia that barely improves with oxygen, with diffuse lung involvement. Which condition?
    Show answer
    ARDS. Widespread inflammation makes alveolar capillaries leak, flooding alveoli → diffuse, refractory hypoxemia.
  2. SATA: Which raise PE risk? (a) DVT, (b) prolonged immobility, (c) daily walking, (d) recent femur fracture, (e) smoking/oral contraceptives.
    Show answer
    a, b, d, e. Walking (c) reduces risk.
  3. Why is a post-op patient at risk for atelectasis, and what prevents it?
    Show answer
    Pain and shallow breathing let alveoli collapse. Deep breathing, coughing, and incentive spirometry keep them open.
🩺 Clinical scenario
Three days after knee surgery, a patient becomes suddenly short of breath with sharp chest pain, coughs blood, SpO₂ 85%. What emergency, and its origin?

Pulmonary embolism — a clot from a leg DVT (post-op immobility) lodged in a pulmonary artery. Blocked perfusion creates dead space → sudden dyspnea, pleuritic pain, hemoptysis, hypoxemia.
TOPIC 11 🩹 Chest Trauma & Pneumothorax HIGH YIELD▼

Chest trauma disrupts ventilation (and sometimes circulation). Know the mechanics of each.

InjuryPatho & signs
Flail chestMultiple ribs broken in multiple places → loose segment moves paradoxically (inward on inspiration) → decreased ventilation
PneumothoraxAir in the pleural space separates visceral & parietal pleura → the lung collapses. Hemothorax = blood in the pleural space
Tension pneumothoraxTrapped air keeps building → shifts the mediastinum and compresses the heart/great vessels. MEDICAL EMERGENCY. S/S: hypotension, tachycardia, tracheal deviation AWAY from the affected side, JVD, absent breath sounds on one side
🧠 Memory hooks
Flail chest = paradoxical movement (sucks IN on inspiration). Pneumothorax = air in pleura → collapse. Tension = compresses the heart → hypotension + trachea AWAY + JVD — decompress now.
⚠ Common confusion
A simple pneumothorax is a stable collapsed lung. A tension pneumothorax adds hemodynamic collapse — look for hypotension, JVD, and tracheal deviation away.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. After a crash, part of the chest wall moves inward during inspiration. Which injury, and what is this called?
    Show answer
    Flail chest — multiple ribs broken in multiple places create a free segment with paradoxical movement, reducing ventilation.
  2. A trauma patient has hypotension, absent left breath sounds, JVD, and the trachea deviated right. Which emergency?
    Show answer
    Tension pneumothorax (left side). Trapped air compresses the heart/great vessels and pushes the trachea away — needs immediate decompression.
  3. What is a hemothorax?
    Show answer
    Blood in the pleural space (vs air in a pneumothorax) — it also compresses the lung and impairs ventilation.
🩺 Clinical scenario
A stabbing victim becomes rapidly hypotensive and tachycardic with distended neck veins, no right breath sounds, and the trachea shifted left. What is happening and why is it lethal?

Tension pneumothorax. Air enters the pleural space and can't escape; the building pressure collapses the lung and compresses the heart/great vessels so blood can't return — obstructive shock. Requires immediate decompression.

🎯 Exam Cheat Sheet — Night-Before Review

Module 11 — Renal Disorders

  • Labs: creatinine UP = GFR DOWN; BUN rises with declining function. End-stage GFR < 15.
  • UTI: lower = cystitis (bladder; frequency/dysuria); upper = pyelonephritis (kidney; flank pain + fever).
  • NephrOtic = prOtein leak → proteinuria >3.5 g, hypoalbuminemia, edema, hyperlipidemia (diabetes/lupus). NephrItic = Inflammation → hematuria (cola urine), HTN (post-strep).
  • AKI: Pre-renal (↓flow — dehydration/shock), Intra-renal (ATN, nephrotoxins, GN), Post-renal (obstruction — BPH). Phases: Oliguric → Diuretic → Recovery.
  • CKD: #1 causes diabetes & hypertension. UP: Cr/BUN, K⁺, H⁺ (acidosis), phosphate. DOWN: GFR, calcium, vitamin D, EPO (→ anemia).
  • Stones = colicky flank→groin pain (hydrate to prevent). PKD = inherited cysts + HTN. Bladder cancer = painless hematuria. Incontinence: overflow / stress / urge.

Module 12 — Respiratory

  • 3 steps: Ventilation (obstructive), Diffusion (membrane/fluid), Perfusion (blood flow). Shunt = blood no air; Dead space = air no blood (PE).
  • Lung sounds: crackles = fluid; wheeze = tight airways; rhonchi = mucus; stridor = upper airway.
  • COPD: chronic bronchitis (mucus, cough, hypercapnia) + emphysema (barrel chest, air trapping). Hypoxic drive — don't over-oxygenate.
  • Asthma = bronchoconstriction + inflammation (reversible). CF = autosomal recessive thick mucus. Pneumoconiosis = dust. OSA/chronic lung dz → pulmonary HTN → right HF.
  • Pneumonia = bacterial, localized, fluid-filled alveoli. Acute bronchitis = viral, self-limiting. Flu = upper epithelium; COVID = alveoli via ACE-2.
  • ARDS = diffuse inflammation (sepsis/burns/trauma) → leaky alveoli, refractory hypoxemia. PE = clot → dead space. Atelectasis = collapsed alveoli (post-op).
  • Trauma: flail chest = paradoxical movement; pneumothorax = air in pleura; tension pneumothorax = hypotension + JVD + trachea deviates AWAY (emergency).

📚 Glossary

GFR — glomerular filtration rate; the main measure of kidney function (falls with disease).

Creatinine / BUN — waste markers that rise as kidney function declines.

Cystitis / Pyelonephritis — bladder infection / kidney infection.

Nephrotic syndrome — protein-leaking glomerulus → proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, edema, hyperlipidemia.

Nephritic syndrome — inflamed glomerulus → hematuria, hypertension (often post-strep).

Acute tubular necrosis — the most common intra-renal AKI; tubular cell death.

Hydronephrosis — kidney swelling from obstructed urine backing up.

CKD — chronic kidney disease; progressive nephron loss (diabetes, hypertension).

Nephrolithiasis — kidney stones.

Ventilation / Diffusion / Perfusion — the three steps of respiration.

V/Q mismatch — imbalance of ventilation and perfusion (shunt vs dead space).

Hypoxemia / Hypoxia — low oxygen in the blood / in the tissues.

COPD — chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (chronic bronchitis + emphysema).

Hypoxic drive — reliance on low O₂ to stimulate breathing in chronic COPD.

Pneumonia — localized alveolar infection filling air sacs with fluid.

ARDS — diffuse alveolar inflammation/edema → refractory hypoxemia.

Pulmonary embolism — a clot blocking a pulmonary artery (dead space).

Atelectasis — alveolar collapse (often post-op).

Tension pneumothorax — trapped pleural air compressing the heart — an emergency.

🎯 Final Study Tips

  • Renal: watch GFR & creatinine — they move in opposite directions. Know pre/intra/post-renal AKI and the CKD lab pattern cold.
  • Lungs: categorize by the broken step — ventilation, diffusion, or perfusion. That alone predicts most symptoms.
  • Lock in the opposite pairs: nephrotic vs nephritic, cystitis vs pyelonephritis, chronic bronchitis vs emphysema, shunt vs dead space, crackles vs wheezes vs rhonchi.
  • Know the emergencies: tension pneumothorax, pulmonary embolism, ARDS, and obstructive AKI.
  • Creatinine/BUN = kidney-function markers (rise as GFR falls). Lung sounds: crackles = fluid, wheezes = tight airways, rhonchi = mucus.
  • Practice SATA carefully — read each option as its own true/false statement; partial credit usually isn't given.
  • Use the cheat sheet the night before AND the morning of the exam, then self-test with the answers hidden. Format: 50 questions, Respondus LockDown Browser — prep your space ahead of time.
