1 GI Motility and Secretion: Which Cell Makes What, and What Its Loss Causes ▶
Key idea — Every gastrointestinal symptom in this module traces back to one of three failures: a cell stopped making its secretion, a nerve stopped telling muscle when to contract or relax, or a barrier stopped protecting the epithelium from what the epithelium itself produces. Learn the cell, its product, and its feedback loop, and the disease writes itself.

The gastric gland is best understood as a small factory with five workers. Parietal cells in the body and fundus make hydrochloric acid through the H+/K+ ATPase proton pump, and they also make intrinsic factor — one cell, two products, which is why a single autoimmune attack produces both achlorhydria and B12 deficiency. Chief cells release pepsinogen, which acid itself cleaves into active pepsin. Enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells release histamine, the final common amplifier of acid secretion. G cells in the antrum release gastrin into the blood. D cells release somatostatin, the universal off-switch. Acid secretion has three inputs — acetylcholine from the vagus, gastrin from G cells, and histamine from ECL cells — and because histamine amplifies the other two, H2 blockers blunt all three pathways indirectly while proton pump inhibitors shut the pump itself and therefore act more completely.

The feedback loop is the single most testable piece of physiology here. Acid arriving in the antrum stimulates D cells to release somatostatin, which switches off the G cells. Acid is therefore the brake on its own production. Anything that removes acid removes the brake, and gastrin rises. This is why the same laboratory value — a high serum gastrin — means completely different diseases depending on the gastric pH sitting next to it.

Pearl — Never interpret a serum gastrin without the gastric pH beside it. High gastrin with a very low pH means an autonomous source that is ignoring feedback — a gastrinoma. High gastrin with a high pH means the G cells are shouting appropriately at a stomach that can no longer make acid — atrophic gastritis, or simply a patient taking a proton pump inhibitor. Same number, opposite diseases.

Below the stomach, chyme acidity and fat content drive the next layer of control. S cells sense acid and release secretin, which calls for pancreatic and biliary bicarbonate to raise duodenal pH into the range where lipase works. I cells sense fatty acids and amino acids and release cholecystokinin, which contracts the gallbladder, stimulates acinar enzyme release, and slows gastric emptying so the duodenum is not overwhelmed. This is why a patient with Zollinger-Ellison syndrome has not only ulcers but diarrhoea and steatorrhoea: the acid load overwhelms bicarbonate buffering and denatures pancreatic lipase in the lumen.

Memory hook CCK does three C things: Contracts the gallbladder, Calls out the pancreatic enzymes, and Clamps down gastric emptying.

Motility depends on a pacemaker and a wiring diagram. The interstitial cells of Cajal generate slow waves that set the maximum contraction frequency. The myenteric plexus controls motility; the submucosal (Meissner) plexus controls secretion and local blood flow. Between meals the migrating motor complex sweeps residue and bacteria distally roughly every 90 to 120 minutes — the housekeeper wave — and when it fails, bacteria are not swept out and small intestinal bacterial overgrowth follows. Diabetic autonomic neuropathy damages vagal fibres and the ICC network, producing gastroparesis with early satiety, postprandial vomiting of recognisable food eaten hours earlier, and erratic glycaemic control because absorption no longer matches insulin timing.

  • Achalasia: loss of inhibitory NO and VIP neurons in the lower oesophageal sphincter, so the sphincter cannot relax and the body loses peristalsis — dysphagia to solids and liquids from the outset, regurgitation of undigested food, bird-beak tapering on barium.
  • Hirschsprung disease: failure of neural crest migration leaves a distal aganglionic segment that is tonically contracted — failure to pass meconium in 48 hours, with dilated normal bowel proximal to the narrow abnormal segment.
  • Gastroparesis: vagal and ICC injury from diabetes, surgery or medication, with delayed emptying and bezoar risk.
  • Ileus: the enteric nervous system is intact but suppressed — by surgery, opioids, hypokalaemia, sepsis or peritonitis.
  • Bacterial overgrowth: loss of the migrating motor complex or of gastric acid, giving bloating, B12 deficiency (bacteria consume it) and a paradoxically elevated folate (bacteria make it).
Loss of inhibitory NO/VIP neurons → lower oesophageal sphincter cannot relax → food column stalls and the body dilates → regurgitation, weight loss, aspiration risk
Red flagA macrocytic anaemia must have B12 measured before folate is given. Folate will correct the blood picture in B12 deficiency while the spinal cord degeneration silently progresses. New paraesthesia, loss of vibration and proprioception, or an unsteady gait in a patient with atrophic gastritis or after ileal resection is a neurological emergency, not an anaemia to be treated at the next visit.
Red flagDysphagia of rapid onset in an adult over 60, especially with weight loss, is pseudoachalasia from malignancy at the gastro-oesophageal junction until endoscopy proves otherwise. True achalasia declares itself slowly over years.

Q1. A 62-year-old woman with vitiligo and autoimmune hypothyroidism has a macrocytic anaemia and numbness in both feet with loss of vibration sense. Gastric biopsy shows destruction of the oxyntic glands of the body and fundus. Which single cellular loss explains BOTH the anaemia and the neurological findings?

A. Chief cell loss, so pepsinogen is no longer available to digest dietary protein
B. Parietal cell loss, so intrinsic factor is not produced and vitamin B12 cannot be absorbed in the terminal ileum
C. G cell loss in the antrum, so gastrin no longer stimulates acid secretion
D. D cell loss, so somatostatin no longer restrains gastrin release
B is correct. The parietal cell makes two products — acid and intrinsic factor — so a single autoimmune attack causes achlorhydria and B12 malabsorption together. B12 requires intrinsic factor for receptor-mediated uptake in the terminal ileum; without it the patient develops megaloblastic anaemia plus subacute combined degeneration of the dorsal columns and corticospinal tracts. Chief cell loss is real in atrophic gastritis but pancreatic proteases compensate, so it is clinically silent. Neither G cell nor D cell loss causes B12 deficiency, and note the trap: in atrophic gastritis gastrin actually rises, because the loss of acid removes the somatostatin-mediated brake.

Q2. Two patients each have a serum gastrin above 1000 pg/mL. Patient A has a gastric pH of 1.2, multiple ulcers in the distal duodenum and jejunum, and watery diarrhoea with steatorrhoea. Patient B has a gastric pH of 6.8 and a macrocytic anaemia. Which interpretation is correct?

A. Both must have a gastrinoma, because gastrin this high has only one cause
B. Patient A has autoimmune atrophic gastritis and Patient B has a gastrinoma
C. Patient A has an autonomous gastrin source (gastrinoma) ignoring feedback, while Patient B has atrophic gastritis in which loss of acid removes the normal brake on G cells
D. Both have H. pylori, since urease raises pH and therefore raises gastrin in every infected patient
C is correct. Acid is the physiological brake on gastrin, acting through antral D cells and somatostatin. High gastrin persisting in the face of a very low pH means the source is ignoring feedback and is therefore ectopic and autonomous — a gastrinoma, which classically causes multiple ulcers in unusual distal locations plus diarrhoea and steatorrhoea because the acid load inactivates pancreatic lipase. High gastrin with achlorhydria is an appropriate, expected response to an absent brake, and is seen in atrophic gastritis and in any patient on a proton pump inhibitor. The number alone is uninterpretable without the pH.

Q3. Manometry shows absent peristalsis in the distal oesophagus and a lower oesophageal sphincter that fails to relax with swallowing; barium shows a dilated body tapering to a smooth 'bird's beak'. The primary lesion is:

A. Excess acetylcholine released from surviving excitatory neurons
B. Fibrosis and scarring of the sphincter from years of acid reflux
C. Loss of inhibitory myenteric neurons that release nitric oxide and vasoactive intestinal peptide
D. Loss of interstitial cells of Cajal, so slow waves can no longer be generated
C is correct. Sphincter relaxation is not a passive event — it is an active inhibition delivered by myenteric neurons using nitric oxide and VIP. When those neurons are destroyed (idiopathically, or by Trypanosoma cruzi in Chagas disease), the excitatory tone is unopposed and the sphincter stays shut, while the denervated body loses coordinated peristalsis. A peptic stricture from reflux does narrow the distal oesophagus, but it follows a long heartburn history, causes dysphagia to solids only, and leaves peristalsis intact — that is the tempting distractor. Loss of interstitial cells of Cajal disturbs gastric pacemaking and causes gastroparesis, not failure of sphincter relaxation.

Q4. A man with long-standing alcohol-related chronic pancreatitis has bulky, greasy, foul-smelling stools, easy bruising, and difficulty seeing at night. His serum amylase is normal. The best explanation is:

A. Loss of pancreatic ductal cells alone, so bicarbonate is not secreted
B. Loss of pancreatic acinar cells, so lipase output falls below the digestive threshold and the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K are not absorbed
C. Loss of enterocyte lactase, producing an osmotic diarrhoea
D. Loss of duodenal I cells, so cholecystokinin no longer empties the gallbladder
B is correct. Steatorrhoea appears only when lipase secretion falls below roughly 10 per cent of normal, which requires extensive acinar destruction. Because that acinar mass is destroyed and burnt out, the serum amylase is often normal — a normal amylase never excludes chronic pancreatitis. Fat malabsorption carries the fat-soluble vitamins with it, giving night blindness (A) and easy bruising from vitamin K-dependent factor deficiency. Ductal bicarbonate loss genuinely contributes, because an acid duodenum inactivates residual lipase, but by itself it does not explain the vitamin deficiencies. Lactase deficiency gives watery, gassy osmotic diarrhoea, not greasy stool with vitamin deficits.
2 GERD and Barrett Oesophagus: A Barrier Problem That Becomes a Cancer Problem ▶
Key idea — Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease is not an acid-excess disease. Most patients make a normal amount of acid; the failure is mechanical containment. Severity tracks contact time — how long acid, pepsin and bile sit against squamous epithelium that was never designed to meet them.

The antireflux barrier has several components acting together: the intrinsic tone of the lower oesophageal sphincter, the crural diaphragm squeezing it from outside, the acute angle of His acting as a flap valve, and the length of intra-abdominal oesophagus that transmits abdominal pressure to the sphincter rather than working against it. A hiatal hernia is so important because it dismantles several of these at once: the sphincter slides into the chest, separates from the crural pinch, blunts the angle of His, and creates a small acid reservoir above the diaphragm that empties back into the oesophagus with every swallow.

The dominant mechanism of individual reflux episodes is the transient lower oesophageal sphincter relaxation — a vagally mediated relaxation that is not triggered by swallowing and is provoked by gastric distension. That single fact explains most of the management advice: large meals, late meals, obesity, and delayed gastric emptying all increase fundic distension and therefore increase reflux episodes. It also explains why raising the head of the bed, avoiding recumbency after eating, and weight loss work mechanically rather than pharmacologically.

  • Increased intra-abdominal pressure: obesity, pregnancy, ascites, tight garments, straining.
  • Reduced sphincter tone: nicotine, alcohol, chocolate, peppermint, high-fat meals, caffeine, calcium channel blockers, nitrates, anticholinergics, and progesterone in pregnancy.
  • Delayed clearance: reduced salivary bicarbonate (sleep, xerostomia, scleroderma), impaired peristalsis.
  • Delayed gastric emptying: gastroparesis, gastric outlet narrowing, large fatty meals.
  • Anatomical: hiatal hernia, prior surgery at the gastro-oesophageal junction.

Histologically, refluxate widens the intercellular spaces of the squamous epithelium first — this is why patients can have symptoms with a normal-looking endoscopy (non-erosive reflux disease). Persistent injury produces basal zone hyperplasia, elongated vascular papillae, and intraepithelial eosinophils and neutrophils. As injury deepens the ladder of complications climbs: erosive oesophagitis, then ulceration and bleeding, then fibrotic peptic stricture (progressive dysphagia to solids in a patient with a long heartburn history), then metaplasia.

Chronic acid and bile exposure → squamous injury → intestinal metaplasia with goblet cells (Barrett) → low-grade dysplasia → high-grade dysplasia → oesophageal adenocarcinoma

Barrett oesophagus is metaplasia: the stratified squamous epithelium of the distal oesophagus is replaced by intestinal-type columnar epithelium containing goblet cells. Endoscopically it appears as salmon-pink tongues extending proximally above the gastro-oesophageal junction. Metaplasia is an adaptive change — columnar epithelium survives acid far better than squamous — but it is bought at a price, because the new epithelium is a proliferative, genetically unstable population from which dysplasia and then adenocarcinoma can arise. Progression risk is low per year (roughly half a per cent) but cumulative over decades, which is why surveillance endoscopy with biopsy, not symptom control, is what determines outcome.

Pearl — Many patients report that their heartburn improved as Barrett developed. This is not healing — columnar metaplastic epithelium simply lacks the acid-sensitive nerve endings of squamous mucosa. The most dangerous transformation in the oesophagus is often the point at which the patient stops complaining. Treat a change in symptom character in a long-standing refluxer as a reason to look, not a reason to relax.

Red flagAlarm features in a refluxer demand endoscopy rather than another trial of acid suppression: dysphagia, odynophagia, unintentional weight loss, iron deficiency anaemia, overt GI bleeding or melaena, persistent vomiting, a palpable mass, or new-onset symptoms in an older adult. Empirical treatment of alarm symptoms delays a cancer diagnosis into an incurable stage.

Reflux also causes disease outside the oesophagus, because refluxate can reach the pharynx and airway. Chronic cough, hoarseness and posterior laryngitis, adult-onset or worsening asthma, globus sensation, and dental enamel erosion on the lingual surfaces are all recognised extraoesophageal manifestations. In a debilitated or neurologically impaired patient, nocturnal reflux is a genuine aspiration risk and can present as recurrent pneumonia rather than as heartburn at all.

Q1. Endoscopy in a 55-year-old man with 20 years of heartburn shows salmon-coloured tongues of mucosa extending 4 cm above the gastro-oesophageal junction. Biopsy shows columnar epithelium containing goblet cells. This change is best described as:

A. Dysplasia, an established premalignant disordered growth
B. Hyperplasia of the native squamous epithelium in response to injury
C. Metaplasia — replacement of stratified squamous by intestinal-type columnar epithelium, adaptive in the short term but premalignant over time
D. Anaplasia, indicating that invasive carcinoma is already present
C is correct. Metaplasia is the replacement of one differentiated cell type by another that better tolerates the local stress; here squamous epithelium is replaced by intestinal-type columnar epithelium with goblet cells, which survives acid far better. It is adaptive and can regress if the stimulus is removed, but the new proliferative population is genetically unstable and is the substrate for adenocarcinoma. Dysplasia is the next step — disordered, atypical growth that has not yet invaded — and would be reported as such by the pathologist. Anaplasia means complete loss of differentiation and belongs to established malignancy. Hyperplasia is an increase in the number of the same cells, which is not what is described.

Q2. A 58-year-old man with two decades of nightly heartburn reports that his symptoms have actually eased over the past two years, but he now regurgitates food and has lost 6 kg without trying. Why is the improvement in heartburn NOT reassuring?

A. Columnar metaplastic epithelium is far less acid-sensitive than squamous epithelium, so symptoms fade at exactly the point the highest-risk lesion is developing
B. Symptom improvement proves the lower oesophageal sphincter has recovered, so the weight loss must be unrelated
C. Acid suppression eliminates the risk of adenocarcinoma once symptoms resolve
D. Weight loss lowers intra-abdominal pressure and therefore reduces reflux, which explains the whole picture

Acid suppression relieves symptoms and heals oesophagitis, but it does not abolish the cancer risk once metaplasia exists, which is why surveillance rather than symptom control determines outcome.

A. Distal third with long-standing GERD and obesity gives adenocarcinoma; upper and middle thirds with smoking, alcohol, achalasia or a caustic stricture gives squamous cell carcinoma

Barrett is never the precursor of squamous carcinoma — by definition Barrett has already lost its squamous lining.

Q4. Which mechanism accounts for most individual reflux episodes in typical GERD?

A. A permanently hypotensive lower oesophageal sphincter with a resting pressure near zero
B. Transient lower oesophageal sphincter relaxations that are not triggered by swallowing and are provoked by gastric distension
C. Failure of the oesophageal squamous epithelium to secrete a protective mucus layer
D. Acid output that is substantially above the normal range
B is correct. Most patients with GERD have a normal resting sphincter pressure. The dominant event is the transient relaxation — a vagally mediated reflex triggered by fundic distension, unrelated to swallowing — which is precisely why large meals, obesity, delayed gastric emptying and a hiatal hernia matter so much. A permanently hypotensive sphincter does occur, notably in scleroderma, but it is the exception. Acid hypersecretion is not the usual problem: the acid is normal in quantity and simply in the wrong place, which is why the disease is defined by contact time, not by acid output.
3 Peptic Ulcer Disease: Gastric versus Duodenal ▶
Key idea — An ulcer is a local failure of the balance between aggressive factors and mucosal defence, deep enough to breach the muscularis mucosae — that depth is what separates a true ulcer from an erosion, and it is what allows an ulcer to reach an artery or the peritoneal cavity. Two causes dominate, and they attack from opposite directions: H. pylori raises aggression, while NSAIDs remove defence.

Mucosal defence is layered. A mucus-bicarbonate gel holds a pH gradient so that the luminal surface can sit at pH 2 while the epithelial cell surface stays near neutral. Surface phospholipids make the layer hydrophobic. Damaged cells are replaced within minutes by restitution, in which neighbouring cells flatten and migrate across the defect. Underneath all of this, mucosal blood flow removes back-diffused hydrogen ions and delivers the bicarbonate and oxygen the repair needs. Prostaglandin E2 maintains almost all of these layers simultaneously — mucus output, bicarbonate output, blood flow and restitution — which is why a single drug that blocks prostaglandin synthesis can dismantle the whole system.

Helicobacter pylori is a spiral, urease-producing organism that survives in the mucus layer by generating ammonia to buffer its immediate surroundings. Where it settles determines the disease, and this is the highest-yield concept in the topic. Antral-predominant infection inflames the D cells, reducing somatostatin, so gastrin rises, parietal cell mass expands and acid output climbs; the excess acid delivered into the duodenum induces gastric metaplasia in the bulb, which the organism then colonises — the duodenal ulcer pathway. Body-predominant or pangastritis destroys the oxyntic glands instead, producing atrophy, hypochlorhydria and intestinal metaplasia — the gastric ulcer, gastric adenocarcinoma and MALT lymphoma pathway. One organism, opposite acid consequences, decided by geography.

Antral H. pylori → D cell inhibition → somatostatin falls → gastrin rises → acid output rises → acid load into duodenal bulb → gastric metaplasia → colonisation and ulceration

NSAIDs injure by inhibiting COX-1, cutting prostaglandin E2 and stripping mucus, bicarbonate and mucosal blood flow. The effect is systemic, not merely topical, which is the point students most often miss: enteric-coated, rectal and intravenous NSAIDs still cause ulcers because the prostaglandin deficiency is delivered by the bloodstream. Risk multiplies with age over 65, high dose or two NSAIDs together, concurrent corticosteroids, anticoagulants or SSRIs, a prior ulcer, and coexisting H. pylori. Because NSAID ulcers blunt pain perception through the same prostaglandin pathway, an appreciable number of NSAID users present for the first time with bleeding or perforation rather than pain.

Complications follow anatomy exactly. Bleeding is the most common; a posterior duodenal bulb ulcer lies directly against the gastroduodenal artery and produces brisk arterial haemorrhage, while a gastric ulcer on the lesser curve erodes branches of the left gastric artery. Perforation is the province of the anterior duodenal wall, which has nothing behind it but peritoneum: sudden severe pain, a rigid board-like abdomen, absent bowel sounds and free air under the diaphragm. Penetration is a walled-off perforation into an adjacent organ, classically into the pancreas, converting the pain into a constant boring ache radiating to the back. Gastric outlet obstruction results from oedema or scarring near the pylorus and produces vomiting of undigested food and a succussion splash.

Memory hook Anterior perforates (free Air); posterior bleeds (the artery is Behind). Two walls of the same duodenal bulb, two entirely different emergencies.
Memory hook Cushing = Cranium (raised intracranial pressure drives vagal acid hypersecretion). Curling = Charred (burns cause hypovolaemia and mucosal ischaemia). Both are stress-related mucosal disease; the routes are opposite — too much acid versus too little blood.
Pearl — Every gastric ulcer is biopsied and its healing confirmed, because an ulcerated gastric carcinoma is indistinguishable from a benign ulcer on symptoms alone. Duodenal ulcers are not routinely biopsied, because malignancy there is vanishingly rare. If instead you find multiple ulcers, ulcers distal to the duodenal bulb, ulcers that recur after eradication and acid suppression, or ulcers with diarrhoea and steatorrhoea, stop thinking about common causes and think Zollinger-Ellison syndrome.

Q1. A 41-year-old man describes burning epigastric pain that wakes him at 2 a.m. and is relieved within minutes by milk or an antacid. He has gained 2 kg because eating helps. The most likely lesion and its explanation is:

A. A gastric ulcer, because food stimulates acid secretion and worsens the pain
B. A duodenal ulcer, because acid delivered into an empty duodenum causes pain that food buffers
C. Oesophageal adenocarcinoma, because nocturnal symptoms indicate malignancy
D. Acute pancreatitis, because the pain is epigastric and relieved by antacid
B is correct. Duodenal ulcer pain appears 2 to 5 hours after eating and characteristically at night, when acid is secreted into a duodenum that has no food left to buffer it; food or antacid relieves it within minutes, so patients eat more and maintain or gain weight. The gastric ulcer pattern is the mirror image — pain shortly after eating, food avoidance and weight loss — which makes it the tempting distractor, but it does not fit a man gaining weight because eating helps. Oesophageal malignancy presents with progressive dysphagia and weight loss, and pancreatitis produces constant boring pain radiating to the back that is not relieved by antacid.

Q2. An 80-year-old woman taking daily naproxen for osteoarthritis develops a gastric ulcer. H. pylori testing is negative. The mechanism is:

A. Direct stimulation of parietal cell acid hypersecretion by the drug
B. Inhibition of COX-1 with loss of prostaglandin E2, reducing mucus and bicarbonate secretion and mucosal blood flow
C. Autoimmune destruction of parietal cells triggered by the drug
D. Bacterial urease neutralising the protective mucus layer
B is correct. NSAIDs do not raise acid output; they dismantle defence. Prostaglandin E2 sustains mucus and bicarbonate secretion, mucosal blood flow and epithelial restitution all at once, so blocking COX-1 removes several protective layers simultaneously and normal acid becomes sufficient to ulcerate. Because the deficiency is delivered systemically, enteric-coated, rectal and intravenous NSAIDs are not protective — a widespread misconception. Age over 65, corticosteroids, anticoagulants, SSRIs and a prior ulcer all multiply the risk. Urease is the H. pylori mechanism, which has been excluded here.

4 Upper versus Lower GI Bleeding: Localising the Source ▶
Key idea — The dividing line is the ligament of Treitz, and the way you localise a bleed is by asking what the blood has been through, not how much of it there is. Colour, odour and the urea-to-creatinine ratio are all read-outs of transit time and digestion, and a very fast bleed can defeat all three.

Blood entering the stomach is degraded by acid and then by colonic bacteria; haemoglobin is converted to haematin and related pigments, producing stool that is black, sticky, tarry and characteristically foul-smelling. This requires both a volume threshold — usually 50 to 100 mL — and time, roughly 14 hours of transit. Blood that reaches the rectum without that exposure stays red or maroon. So melaena tells you the blood sat in acid and moved slowly; haematochezia tells you it did not — either because the source is distal, or because the bleed was so fast that it swept through before digestion could occur.

The urea-to-creatinine ratio is an elegant piece of physiology. Blood in the small intestine is a large protein meal; it is digested and absorbed, and its nitrogen is converted to urea, raising the BUN. Simultaneously, hypovolaemia reduces renal perfusion and drives proximal tubular urea reabsorption. Both effects push the same way, so a BUN:creatinine ratio above roughly 20 to 30 to 1 with a normal creatinine strongly favours an upper source. A lower GI bleed does not raise urea this way, because the blood never passes through the absorptive small bowel.

Pearl — Haemodynamics change before haemoglobin does. Whole blood is lost with its cells and plasma in the same proportion, so immediately after an acute bleed the haemoglobin concentration is unchanged. It falls only as interstitial fluid shifts in over the following hours, or as soon as crystalloid is given. Tachycardia, a narrowed pulse pressure, orthostatic change and delayed capillary refill are the early findings; a reassuring haemoglobin one hour into a major bleed is a laboratory artefact of timing.

Anchoring on 'red blood means colon' in an unstable patient is the classic and dangerous error. Resuscitate first, scope second.

Upper causes each have a mechanical signature. Peptic ulcer is the most common overall, bleeding when the ulcer base erodes a vessel. Oesophageal varices bleed painlessly, massively, and in the context of chronic liver disease — this is the bleed that kills fastest. A Mallory-Weiss tear is a longitudinal mucosal laceration at the gastro-oesophageal junction produced by forceful retching, so the classic history is several episodes of non-bloody vomiting followed by blood; it is usually self-limited. A Dieulafoy lesion is an abnormally large submucosal artery eroding through otherwise normal mucosa, giving dramatic bleeding with no ulcer to find. An aortoenteric fistula should be considered in anyone with a prior aortic graft who has a small herald bleed before catastrophic haemorrhage.

Lower causes also separate by pattern. Diverticulosis is the most common cause of major lower bleeding: an artery (the vasa recta) is draped over the dome of the diverticulum and ruptures, producing painless, brisk, often large-volume bleeding that usually stops on its own. Angiodysplasia is a degenerative ectasia of mucosal vessels, favours the right colon and the elderly, and characteristically causes slow, recurrent, sometimes occult bleeding; it has a recognised association with aortic stenosis. Ischaemic colitis hits watershed zones such as the splenic flexure and gives pain first, bleeding second, with modest volumes. Haemorrhoids and fissures give small-volume bright red blood on the paper or coating the stool, with pain in the case of a fissure. Slow, occult bleeding presenting only as iron deficiency anaemia in an adult over 50 is colorectal carcinoma until colonoscopy proves otherwise. In a child, painless rectal bleeding should raise Meckel diverticulum with ectopic gastric mucosa.

5 Inflammatory Bowel Disease: Crohn Disease versus Ulcerative Colitis ▶
Key idea — Both diseases are the same fundamental error — a dysregulated mucosal immune response to normal gut flora in a genetically susceptible host — expressed through two different anatomies. Crohn goes DEEP and PATCHY; ulcerative colitis stays SHALLOW and CONTINUOUS. Almost every clinical difference between them is a consequence of depth and distribution, so derive the complications rather than memorising them.

The susceptibility genes differ between the two. NOD2/CARD15 variants are specific to Crohn disease and predict ileal, stricturing disease; ulcerative colitis is associated with a different set of loci including HLA class II and barrier genes. NOD2/CARD15 impairs the intracellular sensing and handling of bacterial products, so the epithelial barrier and innate response fail to keep commensal flora at a respectful distance. The adaptive immune system then mounts a chronic, self-sustaining response against luminal antigens. Both diseases show a bimodal age distribution, peaking in the teens to thirties with a smaller later peak, and both run a relapsing and remitting course. Smoking behaves paradoxically: it worsens Crohn disease and increases post-operative recurrence, but is associated with a lower risk of ulcerative colitis, with UC sometimes presenting shortly after a patient stops smoking.

Crohn's nutritional consequences are dictated by its favourite site. The terminal ileum is the only place that reabsorbs bile salts and the only place with intrinsic factor-B12 receptors, so ileal disease or resection produces B12 deficiency and a depleted bile salt pool. Bile salt loss impairs micelle formation, so fat is malabsorbed, generating steatorrhoea and deficiency of vitamins A, D, E and K. Unabsorbed bile salts that reach the colon also irritate the mucosa and drive a secretory diarrhoea. A shrunken bile salt pool leaves bile lithogenic, so gallstones are more common. And unabsorbed fat binds luminal calcium, which would otherwise have bound oxalate, so free oxalate is absorbed by the colon and calcium oxalate kidney stones follow.

Ulcerative colitis behaves like a disease of the rectum that spreads. Because the rectum is inflamed and non-compliant, patients have urgency, frequency and tenesmus — the sensation of needing to pass stool with an already empty rectum. Bleeding is prominent because the inflamed mucosa is friable and superficially ulcerated. Extent predicts severity: proctitis may be a nuisance, whereas pancolitis is a systemic illness with fever, weight loss and anaemia. Long-standing extensive colitis carries a substantial colorectal cancer risk beginning roughly 8 to 10 years after diagnosis, which is why surveillance colonoscopy with biopsies for dysplasia — not symptom control — determines long-term outcome. Crohn colitis carries a similar risk over comparable amounts of involved colon.

  • Skin: erythema nodosum (tender red shin nodules, tends to track disease activity) and pyoderma gangrenosum (a rapidly enlarging ulcer with a violaceous undermined border, often independent of activity).
  • Eye: uveitis, episcleritis and iritis — eye pain, redness or visual change in an IBD patient is urgent.
  • Joints: peripheral arthritis that follows bowel activity, and axial disease (ankylosing spondylitis, sacroiliitis) that does not.
  • Hepatobiliary: primary sclerosing cholangitis, strongly linked to ulcerative colitis, with a rising alkaline phosphatase and a beaded biliary tree — it also raises cholangiocarcinoma and colorectal cancer risk further.
  • Haematological: anaemia of chronic disease plus iron deficiency, and a genuine hypercoagulable state during flares, so venous thromboembolism must be considered in an unwell IBD inpatient.

Red flagToxic megacolon. A patient with colitis who develops fever, tachycardia, hypotension, abdominal distension with a tympanitic, tender abdomen and a transverse colon dilated beyond 6 cm is on the edge of perforation, which carries very high mortality. Two interventions make it worse and must be avoided: antimotility agents and opioids, which paralyse an already atonic colon, and colonoscopy or barium enema, which insufflate a paper-thin wall. Paradoxical improvement in diarrhoea in an increasingly unwell colitis patient is an ominous sign, not progress.
Pearl — Colectomy cures ulcerative colitis, because the entire disease lives in the colon; it does not cure Crohn disease, which can recur at the anastomosis and anywhere else along the tract. This single fact explains why surgery is offered relatively readily in refractory UC and is used sparingly and conservatively in Crohn disease, where every resection costs bowel length and repeated resections risk short bowel syndrome.

6 The Acute Surgical Abdomen: Appendicitis, Diverticulitis, and Bowel Obstruction ▶
Key idea — Appendicitis, diverticulitis and strangulating obstruction are the same story told at three sites: a lumen is obstructed → pressure rises → venous drainage fails before arterial inflow does → the wall becomes ischaemic → bacteria translocate → the wall perforates. The tempo differs; the sequence never does. Once you know that sequence, the red flags are simply the point at which the wall stops being viable.

Appendicitis

The appendix has a narrow lumen and a single blind end, so any obstruction — a faecolith in adults, lymphoid hyperplasia after a viral illness in children and adolescents, occasionally a tumour in older adults — traps a secreting mucosa. The mucosa keeps producing mucus into a closed space, intraluminal pressure climbs above venous pressure, drainage fails, the wall becomes oedematous and then frankly ischaemic, resident bacteria invade the devitalised wall, and gangrene proceeds to perforation, typically within 48 to 72 hours of symptom onset.

Luminal obstruction → continued mucus secretion into a closed space → intraluminal pressure exceeds venous pressure → wall oedema and ischaemia → bacterial invasion → gangrene → perforation
Pearl — The migration of the pain is the most teachable sign in the abdomen. Early distension is sensed by visceral afferents that enter the cord at T10, and visceral pain is poorly localised, so the patient reports vague, dull, crampy periumbilical pain. Once inflammation becomes transmural and irritates the adjacent parietal peritoneum, somatic fibres take over and the pain becomes sharp, constant and precisely localised to McBurney's point in the right lower quadrant. Anorexia is nearly universal, and the vomiting comes after the pain — the reverse order (vomiting first, then pain) points toward gastroenteritis instead.
  • Rovsing sign: palpation of the left lower quadrant produces right lower quadrant pain — the pain follows the peritoneum, not the hand.
  • Psoas sign: pain on passive hip extension, suggesting a retrocaecal appendix lying against the psoas muscle.
  • Obturator sign: pain on internal rotation of the flexed hip, suggesting a pelvic appendix.
  • Rebound and guarding: parietal peritoneal irritation; involuntary guarding is the more reliable of the two.
  • Laboratory: mild leukocytosis with a left shift; a normal white count does not exclude early appendicitis, and a very high count suggests perforation.

Diverticular disease

Colonic diverticula are pulsion pseudodiverticula: mucosa and submucosa herniate through the muscular wall at the points where the vasa recta penetrate it, because those points are the weakest spots. They are 'pseudo' because they contain no muscularis. High intraluminal pressure drives them, which is why a low-fibre diet with small, hard stool and vigorous segmenting contractions in the narrow, high-pressure sigmoid colon is the classic setting. Diverticulosis is the presence of the pouches; diverticulitis is what happens when one becomes obstructed by inspissated stool, the trapped mucosa becomes inflamed and ischaemic, and it micro-perforates.

Pearl — One organ, two opposite presentations. Diverticulosis bleeds without pain, because the artery is draped over the dome of the pouch. Diverticulitis hurts without bleeding, because inflammation obliterates the vessel rather than opening it. If a student can hold on to that contrast, most diverticular questions solve themselves.

Bowel obstruction: mechanical versus functional

Mechanical obstruction means a physical blockage. In the small bowel, the order of frequency is adhesions from previous surgery, then incarcerated hernias, then malignancy; in the large bowel it is colorectal carcinoma, then volvulus, then diverticular stricture. Functional obstruction, or ileus, means the lumen is open but peristalsis has stopped — after abdominal surgery, with opioids and anticholinergics, in hypokalaemia and other electrolyte disturbance, in sepsis and peritonitis, after spinal injury or retroperitoneal haemorrhage. Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie syndrome) is the colonic version in a debilitated inpatient, in which massive dilation occurs with no mechanical lesion at all.

The physiology of a mechanical obstruction is a fluid problem long before it is a surgical one. Proximal to the block the bowel distends with swallowed air and with its own secretions, and the obstructed gut keeps secreting while losing its capacity to reabsorb — several litres a day can be sequestered. Rising wall tension then drives plasma into the bowel wall itself and out onto the peritoneal surface. All of this fluid is inside the body but outside the circulation: the classic third space. The result is a patient who is intravascularly depleted — tachycardic, oliguric, with a prerenal picture — while total body water may be nearly normal, and who has lost no blood and passed no diarrhoea to explain it. Persistent vomiting of gastric contents adds loss of hydrogen ion, chloride and potassium, producing a hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis maintained by the volume depletion itself — the direct link back to Module 1.

Strangulation is the transition from a plumbing problem to a dying bowel. As distension raises intramural pressure, the thin-walled veins collapse first, so venous outflow is obstructed while arterial inflow continues; the wall becomes engorged and oedematous, which raises the pressure further, and eventually arterial supply fails. The mucosal barrier breaks down, bacteria and endotoxin translocate into the portal blood and peritoneum, and the patient becomes septic. A closed loop obstruction — a volvulus, or a hernia incarcerated at both ends of a loop — carries the highest risk, because there is no proximal escape for the pressure and the mesentery is often twisted at the same time, cutting the blood supply directly.

Red flagSuspect strangulation when the character of the illness changes: colic gives way to constant, unrelenting pain, with fever, tachycardia out of proportion, localised tenderness or peritoneal signs, a rising white cell count, and — critically — a rising lactate with a metabolic acidosis. Note the acid-base pivot: an obstructed patient who was alkalotic from vomiting and becomes acidotic has almost certainly developed ischaemic bowel. This is an operation, not an observation.
  • Proximal small bowel obstruction: early, copious, bilious vomiting; little distension; dehydration and alkalosis develop fast.
  • Distal small bowel obstruction: marked distension, later vomiting that becomes feculent from bacterial overgrowth, more colic.
  • Large bowel obstruction: gross distension and obstipation dominate; vomiting is late or absent.
  • Closed loop with a competent ileocaecal valve: the colon cannot decompress backwards, so the thin-walled caecum distends most and is the usual site of perforation.

7 Bilirubin Handling and the Three Jaundices ▶
Key idea — Jaundice is a plumbing problem in a three-stage pipeline: production of pigment, conjugation inside the hepatocyte, and excretion into bile. Where the block sits determines whether the bilirubin backing up into blood is fat-soluble (unconjugated) or water-soluble (conjugated), and that single physical property predicts the urine color, the stool color, and the enzyme pattern. If you can reason from solubility, you never have to memorize the tables.

The normal bilirubin pipeline

Red cells live about 120 days. When they are removed, splenic and hepatic macrophages strip the heme ring and convert it to biliverdin and then to unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin. This molecule is lipid-soluble and travels through plasma bound tightly to albumin, which means two things at once: it can cross lipid membranes including the blood-brain barrier, and it cannot be filtered by the glomerulus, so it never appears in urine. The hepatocyte takes it up and the enzyme UDP-glucuronosyltransferase attaches glucuronic acid, producing conjugated (direct) bilirubin, which is now water-soluble. Conjugated pigment is actively pumped into the bile canaliculi, travels down the biliary tree, is stored and concentrated in the gallbladder, and is released into the duodenum after a meal. Colonic bacteria then reduce it to urobilinogen; most becomes stercobilin, which is what makes stool brown. A small fraction of urobilinogen is reabsorbed, recirculates through the liver, and a trace is excreted by the kidney as urobilin, which is what makes urine yellow.

Heme → unconjugated bilirubin (albumin-bound) → hepatocyte conjugation → bile → gut bacteria → stercobilin (brown stool) + urobilinogen (yellow urine)
Pearl — Conjugated means water-soluble, and water-soluble means the kidney can dump it. So dark tea- or cola-colored urine only occurs when conjugated bilirubin is in the blood. A patient who is visibly yellow but whose urine is a normal straw color has an unconjugated problem. Any answer choice that pairs unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia with bilirubin in the urine is internally impossible.

Prehepatic jaundice: too much pigment arriving

Here the liver is healthy but is being flooded. Sickle cell crisis, G6PD deficiency, hereditary spherocytosis, autoimmune hemolysis, a hemolytic transfusion reaction, or resorption of a large hematoma all deliver heme faster than the conjugating enzyme can keep up. Unconjugated bilirubin rises. Because it is albumin-bound, urine bilirubin is negative. Because more pigment does eventually reach the gut, more urobilinogen is made and reabsorbed, so urine urobilinogen goes up and stool is normal or even darker than usual. The supporting labs are the hemolysis panel: high LDH, low haptoglobin, high reticulocyte count, and anemia. In a neonate, immature UGT activity plus a physiologically higher red cell turnover makes this the common form of jaundice, and the danger is that lipid-soluble unconjugated bilirubin crosses the immature blood-brain barrier and deposits in the basal ganglia, causing kernicterus.

Intrahepatic jaundice: the hepatocyte itself is sick

Viral hepatitis, alcoholic hepatitis, acetaminophen toxicity, drug reactions, and cirrhosis damage the cell that does both the uptake and the export. The result is a mixed picture: uptake and conjugation are impaired so unconjugated bilirubin rises, but the injured cell also leaks already-conjugated pigment back into the sinusoids, so conjugated bilirubin usually rises more. The signature is enzymatic: ALT and AST climb into the hundreds or thousands because those enzymes live in the hepatocyte cytoplasm and spill out when cells die, while ALP stays relatively modest. Urine bilirubin is positive and the urine darkens. Stool is normal to pale depending on how much bile still gets out.

Posthepatic jaundice: the pipe is blocked

A stone in the common bile duct, a carcinoma of the pancreatic head, a biliary stricture, or cholangiocarcinoma stops conjugated bile from reaching the gut, so it refluxes back into the blood. Conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble, so it is filtered freely and the urine turns dark. Nothing reaches the colon, so no stercobilin is made and the stool becomes pale, putty-colored and greasy (acholic steatorrhea), and urine urobilinogen disappears. Without bile salts, fat and the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K are malabsorbed, so the prothrombin time lengthens from vitamin K deficiency, which unlike hepatocellular failure will correct with vitamin K. Bile salts deposited in skin cause intense pruritus. The enzyme signature is cholestatic: ALP and GGT rise markedly and out of proportion to ALT and AST, and GGT is what confirms the ALP is coming from bile duct epithelium rather than bone.

8 Cirrhosis: Portal Hypertension Versus Hepatocellular Failure ▶
Key idea — Every single sign of cirrhosis traces back to one of exactly two consequences of fibrosis: blood cannot get THROUGH the liver (portal hypertension) or the liver cannot DO its jobs (hepatocellular or synthetic failure). If you sort each finding into one of those two buckets, you never have to memorize a list — you derive it. A handful of findings, notably ascites and encephalopathy, sit in both buckets, and knowing which ones those are is usually the exam question.

How the architecture fails

Chronic injury from alcohol, hepatitis B or C, or metabolic fatty liver disease activates the hepatic stellate cells that sit in the space of Disse. Normally quiet vitamin A storage cells, they transform into contractile myofibroblasts and lay down dense collagen. Two things happen simultaneously. First, the sinusoids are stiffened and the regenerating hepatocytes reorganize into regenerative nodules that compress the vasculature, so resistance to portal blood flow rises. Second, functional hepatocyte mass is progressively replaced by scar, so synthetic and detoxifying capacity falls. Compounding the first problem, splanchnic arterioles vasodilate under the influence of nitric oxide, driving more blood into an already obstructed portal system. Cirrhosis is therefore irreversible architectural distortion, not simply dead cells.

Chronic injury → stellate cell activation → collagen in the space of Disse → regenerative nodules → increased portal resistance + lost hepatocyte mass → two failure modes

Failure mode 1: blood cannot get through (portal hypertension)

The portal vein normally runs at low pressure. When the hepatic venous pressure gradient exceeds about 5 mmHg, portal hypertension exists; above roughly 10 mmHg it becomes clinically significant, and above about 12 mmHg varices are at real risk of bleeding. Blood that cannot pass through the liver seeks out any pre-existing connection between the portal and systemic circulations. Those portosystemic collaterals are thin-walled, unsupported veins never designed for that pressure, and each one produces a recognizable sign. Meanwhile the spleen, which drains into the portal system, becomes congested and enlarges, and an enlarged spleen sequesters and destroys blood cells.

Failure mode 2: the liver cannot do its jobs (hepatocellular failure)

The liver synthesizes albumin and nearly all clotting factors, conjugates and excretes bilirubin, metabolizes hormones and drugs, converts ammonia to urea, stores glycogen and performs gluconeogenesis, and houses the Kupffer cells that filter portal blood. Lose hepatocyte mass and you lose each of those functions in turn. Because factor VII has the shortest half-life of the clotting factors, the prothrombin time and INR are the earliest and most sensitive markers of failing synthesis; albumin, with a half-life of about 20 days, moves more slowly and reflects chronicity. Estrogen that is normally cleared by the liver accumulates, producing the classic feminization findings in men.

9 Hepatic Encephalopathy and the Ammonia Story ▶
Key idea — Hepatic encephalopathy is what happens when nitrogenous waste generated in the gut reaches the brain instead of the urea cycle. Cirrhosis breaks that route in two independent ways at once: there are too few functioning hepatocytes to convert ammonia to urea, and portosystemic shunts physically route portal blood around the liver. Understanding it as a bypass problem, not just a "high ammonia" problem, explains both the precipitants and the treatment.

Colonic bacteria deaminate dietary and secreted protein, producing ammonia that is absorbed into the portal vein. In a healthy person, essentially all of it is extracted on first pass through the liver and converted to urea for renal excretion. In cirrhosis, blood shunts through collaterals and the residual hepatocytes cannot keep up, so ammonia and other gut-derived neurotoxins such as mercaptans, short-chain fatty acids and false neurotransmitters reach the systemic circulation. Ammonia is small and lipid-permeable and crosses the blood-brain barrier readily.

Inside the brain, astrocytes are the only cells that can detoxify ammonia. Glutamine synthetase combines ammonia with glutamate to form glutamine. Glutamine is osmotically active, so it draws water into the astrocyte, which swells; pathologically these are called Alzheimer type II astrocytes. The consequences are low-grade cerebral edema, disturbed astrocyte support of neurons, depletion of glutamate as an excitatory transmitter, and enhanced GABA-ergic inhibitory tone. Clinically that produces a fluctuating, reversible impairment of consciousness and motor control rather than a fixed focal deficit — which is precisely how it is distinguished from stroke.

Gut protein → bacterial ammonia → portal blood shunted past the liver → ammonia crosses the blood-brain barrier → astrocyte glutamine accumulates → astrocyte swelling and cerebral edema → encephalopathy

Precipitants: something almost always tipped the patient over

  • Gastrointestinal bleeding, the single most important precipitant — a liter of blood in the gut is an enormous protein meal that colonic bacteria convert to ammonia
  • Infection of any kind, especially spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, which increases catabolism and nitrogen load
  • Constipation, which lengthens the contact time between bacteria and luminal protein
  • Dehydration and overdiuresis, which concentrate toxins and reduce renal ammonia clearance
  • Hypokalemia, which drives renal ammoniagenesis, and metabolic alkalosis, which shifts the equilibrium from ionized NH4+ toward diffusible NH3 that can cross membranes and the blood-brain barrier
  • Sedatives, opioids and benzodiazepines, which are both poorly metabolized by a failing liver and additive to enhanced GABA-ergic tone
  • Azotemia and renal failure, which increase urea delivery to gut bacteria that hydrolyze it back to ammonia
  • A TIPS procedure or the progressive enlargement of spontaneous portosystemic shunts
Memory hook Precipitants sort into "more nitrogen in" (GI bleed, high protein load, azotemia, constipation), "less clearance out" (dehydration, renal failure, worsening liver function), and "brain more sensitive" (sedatives, hypokalemia with alkalosis). Any new confusion in a cirrhotic should trigger a hunt through all three columns.

Why lactulose works

Lactulose is a nonabsorbable disaccharide that passes intact to the colon, where bacteria ferment it into lactic and acetic acid. Acidifying the colonic lumen shifts the ammonia equilibrium toward NH4+, the charged ammonium ion, which cannot diffuse across the gut wall and is therefore trapped and excreted. The osmotic load simultaneously speeds transit, giving bacteria less time to produce ammonia and physically removing nitrogen from the gut. The therapeutic target is two to three soft stools per day; too few means underdosing, and too many risks dehydration and hypokalemia, which paradoxically worsen encephalopathy. Rifaximin works by a different route: it is a nonabsorbed antibiotic that reduces the population of urease-producing colonic bacteria, and it is typically added to lactulose rather than replacing it.

Pearl — The serum ammonia level correlates poorly with the depth of encephalopathy, because what matters is intracerebral glutamine, not plasma nitrogen. Treat the patient, not the number. A normal ammonia does not exclude encephalopathy, and a high ammonia in an alert patient does not require escalation. The diagnosis is clinical, made by exam and by excluding other causes of altered mental status.
Pearl — The old teaching of severe protein restriction has been abandoned. Cirrhotic patients are already catabolic and sarcopenic, and muscle is a major extrahepatic site of ammonia disposal, so starving them of protein removes the very tissue that helps clear ammonia. Current practice maintains adequate protein, often favoring vegetable and dairy sources.
Red flagNew confusion in a cirrhotic is never simply "his usual encephalopathy" until you have looked for the trigger: check for occult GI bleeding with a rectal exam, tap the ascites for spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, review sedative and diuretic doses, and check potassium, sodium and renal function. Grade III to IV encephalopathy threatens the airway from loss of protective reflexes, and a rapidly rising INR alongside encephalopathy in a previously healthy liver signals acute liver failure and a transplant referral.

10 Viral Hepatitis A, B and C ▶
Key idea — The hepatitis viruses are only weakly cytotoxic on their own. Nearly all of the hepatocyte destruction is immune-mediated: cytotoxic T lymphocytes recognize viral antigen displayed on infected hepatocytes and kill them. That single fact explains the central paradox of the topic — a vigorous immune response produces a sicker, more jaundiced patient who clears the virus, while a weak response produces a nearly asymptomatic patient who becomes a chronic carrier. It is why a neonate infected with hepatitis B has almost no acute illness and a roughly 90 percent chance of lifelong infection.

Hepatitis A

A non-enveloped RNA picornavirus spread by the fecal-oral route: contaminated water, undercooked shellfish, food handled by an infected person, daycare centers, travel to endemic regions. Incubation is about two to six weeks. Because the virus is shed in stool in the two weeks before symptoms begin, transmission is largely complete by the time the patient turns yellow. Hepatitis A causes an acute, self-limited illness and never becomes chronic and never causes cirrhosis, though it can rarely be fulminant in older adults or those with pre-existing liver disease. IgM anti-HAV confirms acute infection; IgG anti-HAV means past infection or vaccination and confers lifelong immunity. A killed-virus vaccine exists. Hepatitis E behaves similarly, is fecal-oral and self-limited, but carries a strikingly high mortality when it strikes a pregnant woman in the third trimester.

Hepatitis B

The only DNA virus among the hepatitides, transmitted through blood, sexual contact and perinatal exposure — needle sticks, shared injection equipment, unscreened transfusion, and vertical transmission at delivery. Incubation is long, roughly one to six months, so the exposure is often forgotten. About 5 to 10 percent of infected adults fail to clear it and become chronic, but that figure rises to roughly 90 percent in infants infected at birth. Chronic infection carries the risk of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, and hepatitis B is distinctive in that it can cause hepatocellular carcinoma without first causing cirrhosis, because the viral DNA integrates into the host genome and acts as an oncogenic insertion. Extrahepatic immune-complex disease, such as polyarteritis nodosa and membranous nephropathy, can accompany it. The vaccine is recombinant surface antigen only, which is the key to reading serology.

A blood-borne RNA flavivirus; injection drug use is now the dominant route, with transfusion before 1992 and needle-stick injury as historical and occupational sources. That same variability is why there is no hepatitis C vaccine. Over decades chronic infection produces cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, and extrahepatic manifestations include mixed cryoglobulinemia, membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis and porphyria cutanea tarda. Direct-acting antiviral regimens now cure well over 95 percent of cases in eight to twelve weeks, so the emphasis has shifted decisively to screening and detection.

The clinical course of an acute attack

Acute viral hepatitis moves through three phases. The prodromal phase brings malaise, low-grade fever, myalgia, anorexia, nausea and, classically, a sudden aversion to cigarettes in smokers; right upper quadrant discomfort reflects a stretched hepatic capsule. The icteric phase follows as bilirubin rises: jaundice, dark urine, pale stool, pruritus, and often a paradoxical improvement in the systemic symptoms. The convalescent phase brings resolution of jaundice with lingering fatigue for weeks. Laboratory hallmarks are ALT and AST in the hundreds to thousands with ALT usually exceeding AST, a pattern that reverses in alcoholic hepatitis where AST exceeds ALT by more than two to one and the transaminases rarely exceed 400.

11 Gallbladder Disease and Pancreatitis ▶
Key idea — Two organs share one exit. The common bile duct and the main pancreatic duct converge at the ampulla of Vater before emptying into the duodenum, so a single gallstone can produce entirely different diseases depending on exactly where it lodges: the cystic duct gives you biliary colic or cholecystitis, the common bile duct gives you obstructive jaundice or cholangitis, and the ampulla gives you pancreatitis. Locate the stone and the syndrome follows.

Why stones form

About 80 percent of stones are cholesterol stones, which form when bile becomes supersaturated with cholesterol relative to the bile salts and phospholipids that keep it in solution, and when gallbladder hypomotility gives the crystals time to nucleate and grow. Estrogen increases hepatic cholesterol secretion into bile and progesterone slows gallbladder emptying, which is why female sex, pregnancy, and oral contraceptives all raise risk. Obesity raises biliary cholesterol, while rapid weight loss, prolonged fasting and total parenteral nutrition cause stones through stasis, because a gallbladder that is never stimulated to contract is never emptied. Pigment stones come in two flavors: black stones form from chronic hemolysis, sickle cell disease and cirrhosis, where excess unconjugated bilirubin precipitates as calcium bilirubinate; brown stones form in infected or parasitized ducts where bacterial enzymes deconjugate bilirubin in situ. Crohn disease and ileal resection cause stones by interrupting the terminal-ileal reabsorption of bile salts, depleting the pool that keeps cholesterol soluble.

Memory hook The classic 4 F's — Female, Fat, Forty, Fertile — still works, but add the mechanism-based ones the exam actually tests: Fasting and rapid weight loss (stasis), Family history, and Native American ancestry. And remember the counterintuitive one: crash dieting causes gallstones rather than preventing them.

The spectrum of biliary obstruction

Biliary colic is the mildest end and is a misnomer — the pain is steady rather than colicky, because it comes from a distending viscus rather than peristaltic waves. A fatty meal releases cholecystokinin, the gallbladder contracts against a stone temporarily wedged in the cystic duct, pressure rises, and the pain builds over an hour and then subsides once the stone falls back. Nothing is inflamed, so there is no fever and the labs are normal. If the stone stays put, the trapped bile becomes an irritant, the wall becomes inflamed and edematous, secondary bacterial infection follows, and the picture converts to acute cholecystitis. Ultrasound is the first imaging test throughout: it detects stones, wall thickening and pericholecystic fluid, and it is fast, cheap and radiation-free.

Acute pancreatitis: the gland digests itself

The pancreas manufactures enzymes powerful enough to dissolve tissue, so it stores them as inactive zymogens and does not activate them until they reach the duodenal lumen. Acute pancreatitis begins when trypsinogen is prematurely converted to trypsin inside the acinar cell. Trypsin is the master switch: once active, it activates chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, prophospholipase A2 and the kallikrein-kinin cascade. Elastase digests vessel walls and causes hemorrhage, lipase and phospholipase digest fat, and the released inflammatory mediators produce a systemic inflammatory response. The two dominant causes, together accounting for about 80 percent, are gallstones obstructing the ampulla and alcohol, which increases the protein concentration of pancreatic secretions and has direct toxic effects on acinar cells.

Severity is judged by organ failure, hemodynamics, oxygenation and imaging, not by the enzyme number.

Chronic pancreatitis

Repeated injury, most often from sustained alcohol use, replaces acinar tissue with fibrosis, calcification and ductal strictures. The clinical sequence follows the anatomy: the exocrine pancreas, which is 80 to 90 percent of the gland's mass, fails first, so patients develop steatorrhea (bulky, greasy, foul-smelling stools that float), weight loss despite adequate intake, and deficiencies of the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K. The endocrine islets are a small fraction of the gland and fail later, producing a brittle diabetes that is difficult to control because glucagon-secreting cells are lost alongside the beta cells. Pain is chronic epigastric and back pain that may paradoxically diminish as the gland burns out. The counterintuitive laboratory point is that amylase and lipase are often normal in chronic pancreatitis, because so few acinar cells remain to release them; diagnosis rests on imaging showing calcifications and ductal changes, and on fecal elastase testing.

Red flagIn acute pancreatitis, hypotension, tachycardia, oliguria, a falling oxygen saturation, or flank and periumbilical bruising all signal severe necrotizing disease that belongs in an intensive care setting. A rising hematocrit early on is not reassuring — it means hemoconcentration from massive third-spacing and predicts necrosis.

12 Reproductive Pathophysiology: Cycle and PCOS, Prostate, and Torsion ▶
Key idea — Reproductive pathology on this exam reduces to three recurring shapes: a feedback loop that lost its rhythm (polycystic ovary syndrome), a gland growing in the wrong zone (benign prostatic hyperplasia versus prostate cancer), and a blood supply that twisted shut (testicular torsion). Each one is a mechanism you can reason through rather than a list to memorize.

The menstrual cycle in two halves

The follicular (proliferative) phase runs from day 1 to ovulation. FSH from the anterior pituitary recruits a cohort of follicles whose granulosa cells produce estradiol. Rising estradiol does two things: it drives the endometrium to proliferate, thickening the functional layer and regrowing the spiral arteries, and it exerts negative feedback on FSH, which starves the smaller follicles so that only the dominant follicle, with the most FSH receptors, survives. Then the feedback flips. Once estradiol has been sustained at a high level for roughly 48 hours, it switches from negative to positive feedback on the pituitary, producing the LH surge. Ovulation follows the surge by about 36 hours — day 14 in an idealized 28-day cycle.

The luteal (secretory) phase begins when the ruptured follicle luteinizes into the corpus luteum, which secretes progesterone. Progesterone converts the proliferated endometrium into a secretory, glycogen-rich bed capable of supporting implantation, thickens cervical mucus into a barrier, and raises the basal body temperature by about half a degree. The corpus luteum has a fixed lifespan of about 14 days unless rescued by hCG from an implanting embryo. Without hCG it involutes, progesterone and estrogen fall, the spiral arteries constrict, the functional endometrium becomes ischemic and sloughs, and menstruation begins.

FSH → follicle growth → sustained high estradiol → feedback flips positive → LH surge → ovulation → corpus luteum → progesterone → no hCG → luteolysis → progesterone withdrawal → menses
Pearl — Because the corpus luteum's lifespan is fixed at about 14 days, the luteal phase is essentially constant and all cycle-length variation comes from the follicular phase. A woman with a 35-day cycle ovulates around day 21, not day 17. The second implication matters more clinically: progesterone is literally "pro-gestation," and its job is to hold and stabilize the lining. Anything that abolishes ovulation abolishes the corpus luteum, which abolishes progesterone, which leaves the endometrium under unopposed estrogen — continuous proliferation with no organized withdrawal bleed. That single idea is the bridge to PCOS and to endometrial cancer risk.

Polycystic ovary syndrome

PCOS is diagnosed by the Rotterdam criteria, requiring two of three: oligo-ovulation or anovulation, hyperandrogenism (clinical or biochemical), and polycystic-appearing ovaries on ultrasound. The mechanism is a self-reinforcing loop. An increased GnRH pulse frequency preferentially favors LH over FSH secretion, so the LH-to-FSH ratio rises. LH drives the ovarian theca cells to overproduce androgens, while the relatively low FSH means granulosa cells never fully mature a dominant follicle. Follicles arrest at the small antral stage — those are the "cysts," which are actually immature follicles arranged around the periphery, not true cysts. No dominant follicle means no LH surge trigger, no ovulation, and no corpus luteum. Layered on top is insulin resistance, present in a majority of patients and independent of obesity: hyperinsulinemia directly stimulates theca androgen synthesis and simultaneously suppresses hepatic production of sex hormone-binding globulin, so a greater fraction of the circulating testosterone is free and biologically active. Adipose tissue aromatizes androgens to estrone, sustaining the estrogen exposure of the endometrium.

  • Androgenic: hirsutism in a male pattern, acne, androgenic alopecia, and rarely clitoromegaly (frank virilization should prompt a search for an androgen-secreting tumor instead)
  • Metabolic: central obesity, acanthosis nigricans as a visible marker of hyperinsulinemia, dyslipidemia, impaired glucose tolerance and type 2 diabetes, obstructive sleep apnea
  • Reproductive: anovulatory infertility, higher rates of miscarriage, gestational diabetes and preeclampsia

Years of proliferation with no progesterone-driven shedding is the exact setup for endometrial neoplasia, and this is one of the few gynecologic cancers with a clear, preventable, mechanistic pathway.

BPH versus prostate cancer: it is all about zones

The prostate has distinct zones, and the two major diseases arise in different ones — which is why they present so differently. The transition zone wraps directly around the prostatic urethra and is where benign hyperplasia occurs, so BPH compresses the urethra early and announces itself with urinary symptoms. The peripheral zone lies posteriorly against the rectum and gives rise to roughly 70 to 80 percent of adenocarcinomas, so cancer grows away from the urethra and stays silent early, but is palpable on digital rectal exam. BPH is driven by dihydrotestosterone, produced when 5-alpha-reductase converts testosterone within prostatic stromal cells, and it is a hyperplasia of both glandular and stromal elements. It is not premalignant, and having BPH neither causes nor protects against carcinoma; the two simply coexist commonly in aging men.

Testicular torsion

Most patients have a congenital bell-clapper deformity, in which the tunica vaginalis inserts abnormally high on the spermatic cord so that the testis hangs freely inside it like a clapper in a bell rather than being anchored posteriorly. The testis can then rotate on the cord. The sequence matters: thin-walled veins collapse first, so venous outflow is obstructed while arterial inflow continues, producing rapid congestion, massive edema and pain out of proportion to the exam; as pressure builds, arterial inflow is finally choked off and infarction begins. This is why the clock is unforgiving — salvage exceeds 90 percent within 6 hours, falls to roughly 50 percent by 12 hours, and drops below 10 percent after 24 hours. Peak incidence is bimodal, in neonates and in boys aged 12 to 18, where a rapidly growing testis and increased activity raise the risk.

13 Module 1 — Fluid Compartments, Tonicity, Oedema & Electrolytes ▶
Key idea — Sodium decides where the water sits; potassium and calcium decide whether membranes fire. Almost every fluid-and-electrolyte question on the final is secretly asking one of those two things — a volume/osmolality problem, or an excitability problem.

The three compartments and the 60–40–20 rule

About 60% of adult body weight is water. Two-thirds of it is intracellular (ICF) and one-third is extracellular (ECF); the ECF is then split roughly three-to-one into interstitial fluid and plasma. That last slice matters enormously out of proportion to its size, because plasma is the only compartment that perfuses organs. Water crosses these compartments passively, by osmosis, chasing solute — it does not move because we want it to. The compartments therefore stay in osmotic equilibrium at all times, and the only way to shift water is to change the osmolality of one side.

Memory hook 60–40–20: 60% of body weight is water, 40% is intracellular, 20% is extracellular. Split the 20 again into 15% interstitial and 5% plasma. Infants run nearer 70–75% water with proportionally more ECF (which turns over fast), so they dehydrate in hours; the elderly run nearer 50% with less reserve, so they have less margin before a deficit becomes symptomatic.

Tonicity: what a fluid does to a cell

Tonicity is not a property of the bag — it is a prediction about the cell. Compare the infused fluid to the ECF it is entering, then ask which way water must move to equalise the two.

The four causes of oedema

Oedema is excess fluid in the interstitium, and there are only four ways to put it there. Three of them are Starling-force problems at the capillary (the push-out force is too high, the pull-in force is too low, or the wall has become leaky) and the fourth is a drainage problem (the lymphatics that normally mop up the small daily surplus are blocked).

14 Module 1 — Acid–Base Balance and ABG Interpretation ▶
Key idea — There are only two levers on pH. CO2 is the respiratory lever — an acid, controlled by the lungs, adjustable within minutes. HCO3 is the metabolic lever — a base, controlled by the kidneys, adjustable over hours to days. The primary disorder is whichever value is abnormal <i>in the direction that would itself produce the observed pH</i>; the other value moving the opposite way is compensation.

Four moves, every single time

  • Move 1 — pH (7.35–7.45). Below 7.35 is acidosis, above 7.45 is alkalosis. If it is inside the range but not exactly 7.40, note which side of 7.40 it sits on; that side names the primary disorder even when compensation has hidden it.
  • Move 2 — pCO2 (35–45 mmHg). High CO2 means retained acid from hypoventilation; low CO2 means acid blown off by hyperventilation.
  • Move 3 — HCO3 (22–26 mEq/L). Low bicarbonate means acid was gained or base was lost; high bicarbonate means acid was lost or base was gained.
  • Move 4 — match and compensate. Whichever value explains the pH is primary. If the other value has moved in the opposite direction, compensation has begun: pH still abnormal = partially compensated, pH back in range = fully compensated, other value untouched = uncompensated.
Memory hook ROME — Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal. In respiratory disorders the pH and the pCO2 move in opposite directions (pH down, CO2 up = respiratory acidosis). In metabolic disorders the pH and the HCO3 move in the same direction (pH down, HCO3 down = metabolic acidosis).

The anion gap sorts the acidoses

Once you have a metabolic acidosis, one calculation tells you the mechanism: anion gap = Na − (Cl + HCO3), normally about 8–12. A high gap means an unmeasured acid was <i>added</i> to the blood — ketones, lactate, uraemic acids, toxic alcohols — and consumed bicarbonate on the way in. A normal gap means bicarbonate was simply <i>lost</i>, most often through the stool in diarrhoea or through the tubules in renal tubular acidosis; chloride is reabsorbed to fill the vacated anion slot, which is why this is also called a hyperchloraemic acidosis.

Memory hook High-gap acids to recall: ketoacids (DKA, starvation, alcohol), lactate (shock, sepsis, any tissue starved of oxygen), uraemic acids (renal failure) and ingested toxins (salicylates, methanol, ethylene glycol). Normal-gap causes are the two great bicarbonate leaks: diarrhoea below and renal tubular acidosis in the kidney.
DKA → ketoacids accumulate → HCO3 consumed buffering them → anion gap widens → chemoreceptors drive Kussmaul respirations → pCO2 falls as compensation
Red flagA severe asthmatic whose pCO2 has 'normalised' from 26 to 42 is not improving — the respiratory muscles are fatiguing and can no longer sustain the hyperventilation that was keeping the CO2 low. A normal gas in a patient who is visibly working hard is the gas that precedes respiratory arrest. Prepare for intubation rather than reassuring the family.
Pearl — Acid–base and potassium are one system, not two. In acidosis, hydrogen ions move into cells and potassium moves out to preserve electroneutrality, so the serum potassium reads high even when total body potassium is depleted — treat DKA and the potassium will fall sharply as insulin drives it back in. In alkalosis the trade runs the other way and potassium falls. Always read a gas and a potassium together.

16 Cellular Adaptation, Reversible Injury and the Two Ways a Cell Dies ▶

Adaptation is the cell buying time

A cell under stress has three options: adapt, die, or be injured somewhere in between. Adaptation is a reversible, controlled change in size, number or type that lets the cell keep functioning under a new workload. The critical teaching point is that adaptation is not disease — it is successful compensation. It only becomes dangerous when the stimulus persists so long that the adapted cell is itself abnormal (metaplasia drifting into dysplasia) or when the compensating tissue creates a new problem (the hypertrophied ventricle that outgrows its own coronary supply).

Key idea — Remove the stimulus early and the cell walks all the way back to normal. Every adaptation on this list is reversible except the last one, and dysplasia is only reversible while the basement membrane is still intact — once cells breach it, you are no longer describing adaptation, you are describing cancer.

Reversible versus irreversible injury: where the line sits

Almost every cell injury on an exam starts with hypoxia, because oxygen is what oxidative phosphorylation needs to make ATP. When ATP falls, the Na+/K+-ATPase stops running, sodium and water pour into the cell, and the cell swells — the earliest and still-reversible change. The cell also flips to anaerobic glycolysis, lactate accumulates, intracellular pH drops, and ribosomes detach from rough ER so protein synthesis stalls. All of that reverses if perfusion returns. The point of no return is membrane integrity: once lysosomal and mitochondrial membranes rupture and calcium floods the cytosol, activated proteases, phospholipases and endonucleases digest the cell from the inside and nothing brings it back.

Hypoxia → ATP falls → Na+/K+ pump fails → cell swells (REVERSIBLE) → lactic acidosis → massive Ca2+ influx → lysosomal and mitochondrial membrane rupture → enzymatic digestion (IRREVERSIBLE = necrosis)
Pearl — Every cardiac enzyme you have ever ordered is a report on failed membranes. Troponin, CK-MB, ALT, AST and lipase are intracellular proteins — they can only appear in serum if the plasma membrane has been breached. A rising troponin is literally laboratory proof that the injury crossed from reversible to irreversible.

What actually makes a tumour malignant

Students lose points here by grading tumours on size or growth rate. A uterine fibroid can be the size of a grapefruit and a basal cell carcinoma can be 4 mm; neither fact settles the question. 18 Inflammation: The Cardinal Signs, Acute versus Chronic, and Wound Healing ▶

Inflammation is the vascularised tissue's stereotyped response to injury, and it is fundamentally a vascular event before it is a cellular one. Injured cells and resident mast cells release histamine, bradykinin, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, nitric oxide and complement fragments. Two things follow: arterioles dilate, and capillaries and venules become leaky. If a student can hold those two changes in mind, all five cardinal signs can be derived rather than memorised.

19 Module 5 — The Pressurized Brain: ICP, Stroke, TBI and Seizures ▶
Key idea — The skull is a rigid box with a fixed volume, so every neurologic emergency in this module is a variation on one theme: either a volume the cranium cannot accommodate, or a perfusion pressure it can no longer maintain. Neurons hold no fuel reserve — they fail within minutes of losing flow, and the tissue that dies does not come back. Everything you do for a neuro patient is aimed at protecting the tissue that is still salvageable.

Monro-Kellie doctrine: why compensation ends all at once

The cranial vault holds roughly 1,400 to 1,700 mL of three things: brain tissue (about 80%), blood (10%) and cerebrospinal fluid (10%). The Monro-Kellie doctrine states that if any one of the three grows, another must shrink or the pressure climbs. The brain buys time by shunting CSF into the spinal subarachnoid space, absorbing CSF faster, and squeezing blood out of the cerebral veins. Those compensations are finite and non-linear: while they hold, an expanding hematoma barely moves the ICP at all; once they are spent, a few more milliliters send the pressure up a cliff. That is exactly why a head-injured patient can look deceptively stable and then deteriorate in minutes.

Expanding mass → CSF displaced to spinal canal and venous blood squeezed out → compensation exhausted → small added volume produces a steep ICP rise → CPP falls → ischemia → cytotoxic edema → still more volume

Normal ICP is 5 to 15 mmHg, but what actually perfuses the brain is cerebral perfusion pressure: CPP = MAP − ICP, and it must stay above roughly 60 mmHg. Below 50 autoregulation fails and ischemia begins; below 30, infarction is essentially assured. Every ICP intervention is really a CPP intervention — head of bed at 30 degrees with the head midline so the jugular veins drain, no neck or hip flexion, suctioning clustered and brief, fever and pain and seizures treated because each raises metabolic demand, ventilation kept normocapnic (carbon dioxide is the most potent cerebral vasodilator there is), and water pulled out of the brain with mannitol or hypertonic saline. Never hang hypotonic fluid — free water follows the osmotic gradient straight into injured tissue.

Red flagThe earliest sign of rising ICP is a change in level of consciousness — restlessness, confusion, harder to rouse — because the reticular activating system is exquisitely pressure-sensitive. Do not wait for the famous signs. By the time you see Cushing's triad (rising systolic pressure with a WIDENING pulse pressure, bradycardia, and irregular or Cheyne-Stokes respirations), a fixed dilated pupil, or decerebrate posturing, the brainstem is already being compressed and herniation is minutes away.
Pearl — Cushing's triad looks backwards on purpose. In shock, blood pressure falls and heart rate rises; here blood pressure climbs and heart rate falls. The brainstem, starved because ICP is consuming CPP, fires a massive sympathetic surge to raise MAP and force blood back into the skull; the aortic and carotid baroreceptors read that hypertension and reflexly slow the heart, while the compressed medulla disorganizes the respiratory rhythm. A unilateral blown pupil is anatomy talking in the same way: an expanding temporal mass pushes the uncus over the tentorium onto cranial nerve III, whose parasympathetic fibers ride on the outside and die first — so the pupil dilates and stops reacting on the SAME side as the lesion.
Memory hook Posturing: decorticate means arms flexed in toward the core — injury above the midbrain, bad. Decerebrate means arms extended — injury at the midbrain or pons, worse. A patient who moves from decorticate to decerebrate is telling you the damage is descending through the brainstem.

Seizures: excitation outrunning inhibition

A seizure is a paroxysmal, hypersynchronous discharge of cortical neurons — glutamate-driven excitation overwhelming GABA-mediated inhibition in a population of cells that normally fire independently. Focal seizures begin in one hemisphere and may preserve or impair awareness; generalized seizures engage both hemispheres from the outset and always impair it. The tonic-clonic pattern is stiffening (tonic, often with a cry as air is forced through the larynx) then rhythmic jerking (clonic), followed by a postictal period of confusion, headache and heavy sleepiness while the exhausted cortex recovers. That postictal state is itself diagnostic — non-epileptic events usually lack it. Childhood absence seizures are the opposite picture: a blank three-to-ten-second stare with no aura and no postictal phase, routinely mistaken for inattention.

  • A new seizure is metabolic until proven otherwise: hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, hypocalcemia, hypoxia, fever in small children, alcohol or benzodiazepine WITHDRAWAL, stimulant or local anesthetic toxicity, eclampsia. Correct the cause and the seizure stops; anticonvulsants alone will not hold it.
  • Epilepsy means two or more UNPROVOKED seizures — a chronic tendency, not a single event.
  • During the event: nothing in the mouth, no restraint, side-lying to protect the airway, pad and lower to the floor, and note the time plus the pattern of onset — where it starts tells you where the focus is.
  • Check glucose and sodium at the bedside first; EEG identifies the discharge and imaging looks for a structural lesion afterward.
Red flagStatus epilepticus is any seizure lasting 5 minutes or longer, or repeated seizures without full recovery of consciousness in between. Sustained excitotoxicity kills neurons outright, and the systemic cost stacks up fast: hyperthermia, lactic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hypoxia and aspiration. 20 Module 6 — Bone: Osteoporosis versus Osteomalacia, and the Three Fracture Emergencies ▶
Key idea — Bone disease sorts into a quantity problem and a quality problem. Osteoporosis is too little bone that is otherwise normally mineralized; osteomalacia is a normal amount of bone matrix with too little mineral driven into it. That distinction predicts everything downstream — who has pain before the fracture, and what the labs will show. After the fracture, a different clock starts: three complications each strike in their own time window.

The remodeling balance and why estrogen matters

Bone is never static — osteoclasts excavate and osteoblasts refill, with peak bone mass banked by roughly age 30. Osteoblasts actually control their own demolition crew through two signals: RANKL, which activates osteoclasts, and osteoprotegerin, a decoy receptor that soaks RANKL up. Estrogen keeps osteoprotegerin high and osteoclast lifespan short. After menopause that brake is released, resorption outruns formation, and the thin trabeculae of cancellous bone — vertebral bodies, femoral neck, distal radius — are eaten first. Glucocorticoids reach the same endpoint by a different road: they suppress osteoblasts, blunt intestinal calcium absorption and increase urinary calcium loss, which is why steroid therapy expected to run beyond three months demands bone protection from the start.

Pressure injuries: staging by what is exposed

Capillary closing pressure at the skin is roughly 32 mmHg, and lying or sitting on a bony prominence exceeds it easily. Compression collapses the capillaries, tissue goes ischemic, and metabolic waste accumulates. When pressure is relieved, reactive hyperemia flushes the area — redness that BLANCHES under a finger and fades within minutes is healthy compensation, not an injury. Sustained pressure beyond that point causes cell death. Three accomplices accelerate it: shear, where the skeleton slides down in bed while the skin stays put, kinking and tearing deep vessels; friction, which strips epidermis; and moisture from incontinence or sweat, which macerates the barrier. Muscle tolerates ischemia far worse than skin does, so serious injuries frequently begin at the bone-muscle interface and surface only later.

22 Module 7 — Inheritance Patterns, Chromosomal Error, and Recurrence Risk ▶
Key idea — Every pedigree question is really two questions: is the gene on an autosome or on the X, and does one bad copy cause disease (dominant) or do you need two (recessive)? Skipped generations, male predominance, and father-to-son transmission are all downstream consequences of those two switches, so decide the switches first and the pattern names itself.

Reading a pedigree in four moves

Work the chart in a fixed order rather than hunting for a pattern you already have in mind. The order matters because each move eliminates whole categories, and the fourth move is the single most powerful discriminator on the exam.

  • Does it appear in every generation? A vertical, unbroken line of affected people means one mutant allele is enough — dominant. If the trait vanishes for a generation and then reappears among the children of two unaffected parents, those parents were silent carriers — recessive.
  • What is the sex ratio? Roughly equal male and female involvement points to an autosome. A pedigree that is almost entirely male, with the disease travelling through unaffected women, is X-linked recessive: a male has only one X, so a single mutant allele is unopposed.
  • Is there father-to-son transmission? A father gives his son a Y, never his X. Any single male-to-male transmission rules out both X-linked patterns permanently.
  • Check the affected father's daughters. In X-linked dominant disease an affected man transmits to all of his daughters and none of his sons, because every daughter receives his one X. That fan-shaped pattern is unmistakable once you look for it.

Recurrence risk: what carries over to the next pregnancy

Recurrence risk is calculated per pregnancy and has no memory. Three affected children do not use up the 25% for a carrier couple; each conception is an independent roll. For aneuploidy caused by sporadic nondisjunction, recurrence is low — roughly 1% or the mother's age-related risk, whichever is greater — precisely because the error was a one-off mechanical event rather than a transmitted allele. The exception that changes counselling entirely is a parent carrying a balanced Robertsonian translocation: that parent is phenotypically normal but produces unbalanced gametes repeatedly, so recurrence is substantial and parental karyotyping is warranted.

  • Penetrance — the proportion of people with the genotype who show any phenotype. Incomplete penetrance makes a dominant pedigree appear to skip a generation, a classic trap.
  • Expressivity — how severely the disease shows in those who do express it. The same neurofibromatosis mutation may cause a few café-au-lait spots in one relative and disfiguring tumours in another.
  • Anticipation — an unstable trinucleotide repeat expands as it passes through the germline, so each generation is affected earlier and more severely (Huntington disease, fragile X).
  • Mosaicism — a mutation arising after fertilisation, so only some cell lines carry it; findings are patchy and typically milder.
  • Heterozygote advantage — sickle trait resists falciparum malaria, which is why a lethal recessive allele persists at high frequency in endemic regions.
Red flagA newborn with hypotonia, a flat facial profile, and a single palmar crease needs an echocardiogram before discharge. Up to half of infants with trisomy 21 have a congenital heart defect, most often an AV canal, and the murmur can be silent until pulmonary vascular resistance falls over the first weeks — by which time the infant presents in heart failure.

23 Module 8 (Part 1) — Endocrine Feedback Logic, ADH Disorders, and the Thyroid ▶
Key idea — Every endocrine diagnosis reduces to one loop and one rule. The loop: hypothalamus → pituitary tropic hormone → target gland hormone → negative feedback onto both. The rule: never interpret a single hormone level — read the pair. If the target hormone and its tropic hormone move in opposite directions, the target gland itself is diseased (primary). If they move in the same direction, the pituitary or hypothalamus is driving the abnormality (secondary or tertiary).
Hypothalamic releasing hormone → anterior pituitary tropic hormone (TSH, ACTH, LH/FSH) → target gland hormone (T4, cortisol, sex steroids) → negative feedback suppressing both levels above

Work through it once and it becomes automatic. High free T4 with a suppressed TSH means the thyroid is producing hormone on its own and the intact pituitary is trying to shut it off — primary hyperthyroidism. High free T4 with a normal or high TSH is nonsensical for a working feedback loop, so the pituitary must be the culprit (a TSH-secreting adenoma). Low cortisol with a high ACTH means the adrenal cortex has failed while the pituitary shouts at it — primary adrenal insufficiency. Low cortisol with a low ACTH means the shouting has stopped — the lesion is at the top. The same arithmetic works for every axis you will be asked about.

Memory hook Opposite = organ, same = summit. Levels moving in opposite directions incriminate the target organ; levels moving together incriminate the pituitary at the summit of the axis.

ADH and the two water disorders

Antidiuretic hormone acts on V2 receptors of the collecting duct and inserts aquaporin-2 channels into the luminal membrane. It moves water, not sodium — which is the entire key to both diseases. Too much ADH means free water is reabsorbed and retained, diluting the plasma: sodium falls while the urine comes out inappropriately concentrated. Too little ADH (or a duct that cannot answer it) means free water pours out: litres of dilute urine, rising serum sodium and osmolality, and unrelenting thirst that is the only thing keeping the patient compensated. An awake patient with an intact thirst mechanism and access to water can hide diabetes insipidus for a long time; sedate that patient, or intubate them, and the sodium climbs fast.

In Hashimoto thyroiditis, anti-thyroid peroxidase and antithyroglobulin antibodies accompany lymphocytic destruction of follicles; stored hormone leaking from dying cells can cause a brief hashitoxicosis, after which the gland burns out into permanent hypothyroidism.

Diabetes: why type 1 makes ketones and type 2 usually does not

Type 2 diabetes is insulin resistance with a relative deficiency: circulating insulin is inadequate for glucose uptake but is still enough to suppress lipolysis. That single residual function is the whole reason type 2 patients typically do not generate ketoacidosis and instead drift over days into a hyperosmolar state as glucose climbs and osmotic diuresis dehydrates them.

25 Anaemia, Haemolysis, and Sickle Cell Disease ▶
Key idea — Anaemia is never a diagnosis on its own — it is a signal that red cells are being underproduced, lost, or destroyed. The MCV tells you what the marrow was able to build, the reticulocyte count tells you whether the marrow is even trying, and the iron studies tell you whether the raw material or the delivery system is the problem.

Sorting anaemia by cell size (MCV)

Cell size is not cosmetic — it is a fingerprint of the step that failed. A developing erythroblast divides until its cytoplasm is packed with haemoglobin. If haemoglobin cannot be built (no iron, no heme, too few globin chains), the cell keeps dividing while it waits, and the final product is small. If instead DNA synthesis is blocked (B12 or folate), the nucleus cannot keep pace with a cytoplasm that keeps maturing, so the cell grows large before it can divide. If the cells that emerge are normal size, then manufacturing is intact and the problem is loss, destruction, or a marrow that simply is not being told to work.

Sickle cell disease

A single point mutation swaps valine for glutamic acid at position 6 of the beta-globin chain. The resulting HbS behaves normally while oxygenated, but when it gives up its oxygen the abnormal surface lets HbS molecules polymerise into rigid rods that deform the cell into a crescent. Those cells are stiff, sticky to endothelium, and short-lived (10 to 20 days instead of 120), so the patient gets two diseases at once: vaso-occlusion (ischaemic pain crises, stroke, priapism, renal papillary necrosis) and chronic haemolytic anaemia. Anything that increases the deoxygenated fraction or concentrates the haemoglobin inside the cell triggers a crisis: hypoxia, dehydration, acidosis, fever and infection, cold-induced vasoconstriction, and physiologic stress.

Deoxygenation → HbS polymerises → red cell sickles and stiffens → microvascular occlusion + early destruction → ischaemic pain crisis and haemolytic anaemia

Repeated splenic infarction leaves the patient functionally asplenic by early childhood, which removes the body's main defence against encapsulated organisms. This is why vaccination and prompt antibiotics matter so much, and why fever is treated as an emergency rather than a symptom. Hydroxyurea works by raising fetal haemoglobin, which physically dilutes HbS and interrupts polymer formation.

Red flagFever in a sickle cell patient is sepsis until proven otherwise — they are functionally asplenic. And chest pain with fever, hypoxia, and a new pulmonary infiltrate is acute chest syndrome, the leading cause of death in sickle cell disease: it needs oxygen, hydration, analgesia, antibiotics, and often transfusion immediately, not a wait-and-see approach. Also act now on sudden focal neurological deficit (stroke), rapidly enlarging spleen with falling haemoglobin (splenic sequestration), and priapism.

26 Coagulation Patterns, DIC, and Thrombosis ▶
Key idea — Haemostasis happens in two stages, and every bleeding disorder is a failure of one of them. Primary haemostasis is platelets plugging the hole — it fails with mucosal, superficial, immediate bleeding (petechiae, gums, epistaxis, menorrhagia). Secondary haemostasis is the coagulation cascade weaving fibrin through that plug — it fails with deep, delayed, rebleeding-type haemorrhage (joints, muscle, retroperitoneum). Four numbers tell you which arm broke: platelet count, PT/INR, aPTT, and fibrinogen.

What each number is actually measuring

PT/INR tests the tissue-factor (extrinsic) pathway and the common pathway, and it is dominated by factor VII. aPTT tests the contact-activation (intrinsic) pathway — factors XII, XI, IX, and VIII — plus the common pathway. So an isolated aPTT prolongation points at VIII or IX (haemophilia) or heparin; an isolated PT prolongation points at VII (warfarin, vitamin K deficiency, early liver disease); and both prolonged means either the shared final pathway (X, V, prothrombin, fibrinogen) or a global catastrophe such as DIC, liver failure, or massive transfusion. Fibrinogen is the substrate itself and stays normal in almost everything except DIC and end-stage liver failure — which is exactly what makes it so useful. D-dimer is not a clotting test at all: it is a fibrin breakdown fragment, so it tells you clot has been formed and then dissolved.

Disseminated intravascular coagulation

DIC is not a disease; it is a complication of something else — sepsis (the most common), obstetric catastrophe (abruption, amniotic fluid embolism, retained fetus), major trauma or crush injury, burns, malignancy, and severe transfusion reactions. Each floods the circulation with tissue factor, generating thrombin everywhere at once. Microthrombi lodge in the small vessels of the kidneys, lungs, brain, and digits, causing organ ischaemia, and they shear passing red cells into schistocytes. Meanwhile the platelets and clotting factors used to build all that fibrin are consumed faster than the liver can replace them, and plasmin activates to dissolve the clot burden. The result is the paradox students must be able to explain: the patient is clotting and haemorrhaging simultaneously.

Systemic tissue factor release → uncontrolled thrombin generation → widespread microthrombi → consumption of platelets and factors + secondary fibrinolysis → organ ischaemia AND uncontrollable bleeding
Red flagA septic or post-partum patient who starts oozing from every IV site, the gums, and old puncture wounds, with a falling platelet count, prolonged PT and aPTT, falling fibrinogen and a soaring D-dimer, is in DIC. Escalate immediately — and remember the definitive treatment is eliminating the trigger (source control of sepsis, delivery of the fetus); products are supportive only.

Virchow's triad — why thrombi form where they do

Pathological thrombosis needs at least one of three conditions. Stasis: immobility, long flights, atrial fibrillation, varicosities, or a dilated failing ventricle — slow blood lets clotting factors accumulate locally instead of being washed away. Endothelial injury: surgery, indwelling catheters, trauma, smoking, hypertension, hyperglycaemia, or a ruptured atherosclerotic plaque — an intact endothelium is actively anticoagulant, so exposing subendothelial collagen and tissue factor removes that protection. Hypercoagulability: pregnancy and the post-partum period, oestrogen-containing contraceptives, malignancy, factor V Leiden, antiphospholipid syndrome, nephrotic syndrome (antithrombin III is lost in the urine), and the post-operative acute-phase rise in fibrinogen.

Memory hook Virchow's triad: SIT still — Stasis, Injury to endothelium, Thrombophilia. The classic post-operative hip replacement patient collects all three simultaneously, which is precisely why that is the textbook DVT scenario.
Pearl — The triad also explains drug choice. Venous thrombi form in slow, low-shear blood and are fibrin- and red-cell-rich, so you fight them with anticoagulants (heparin, warfarin, DOACs). Arterial thrombi form on ruptured plaque in fast, turbulent flow and are platelet-rich, so you fight them with antiplatelets (aspirin, P2Y12 inhibitors). Same word, two different clots, two different drug classes.
Red flagHeparin-induced thrombocytopenia is the trap: platelets fall 5 to 10 days into heparin therapy, yet the patient clots rather than bleeds, because the antibody-PF4-heparin complex activates the remaining platelets. Stop all heparin, do not transfuse platelets (you are adding fuel), and switch to a non-heparin anticoagulant such as a direct thrombin inhibitor.

From atherosclerosis to infarction

It begins with endothelial injury from hypertension, smoking, hyperglycaemia, or high LDL. Damaged endothelium becomes permeable and sticky: LDL enters the intima and is oxidised, monocytes migrate in and become macrophages that gorge on oxidised LDL to form foam cells, and the resulting fatty streak recruits smooth muscle that lays down a fibrous cap over a soft lipid core. As long as that cap holds, the plaque only narrows the lumen — the patient has symptoms only when demand outruns a fixed supply, which is stable angina and generally requires around 70 percent stenosis. Endothelial injury → LDL entry and oxidation → foam cells and fatty streak → fibrous plaque → cap rupture → platelet-fibrin thrombus → coronary occlusion → myocardial necrosis
Pearl — The plaque that kills is often not the tightest one. Severely stenotic plaques tend to be old, calcified, and stably capped, and they have had time to grow collaterals. The lesion that causes sudden death is frequently a soft, lipid-rich, thin-capped plaque causing only modest stenosis — which is why a patient can pass a stress test and infarct weeks later, and why statins earn their survival benefit by stabilising plaque, not just shrinking it.

When compensation becomes the pathology

A falling cardiac output triggers three ancient reflexes that were designed for haemorrhage, not for a chronically weak pump. Sympathetic activation raises heart rate and vasoconstricts — which raises afterload and myocardial oxygen demand while shortening the diastole in which coronary perfusion actually occurs. RAAS activation retains salt and water, adding preload to a ventricle already stretched past its useful limit, and angiotensin II and aldosterone drive interstitial fibrosis. Remodelling follows: concentric hypertrophy thickens a wall that then outgrows its own blood supply and stiffens, while eccentric dilation stretches sarcomeres past optimal actin-myosin overlap so each fibre generates less force. Every compensation raises wall stress, and wall stress is the thing killing the ventricle.

Falling cardiac output → sympathetic + RAAS activation → more preload, more afterload, remodelling and fibrosis → higher wall stress and oxygen demand → further fall in output (self-reinforcing spiral)
Pearl — This is why the drugs that blunt compensation prolong life — beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, ARBs and ARNIs, aldosterone antagonists, SGLT2 inhibitors — while drugs that simply flog the myocardium harder relieve symptoms without improving survival. It also explains BNP: stretched ventricular myocytes release it as the body's own counter-hormone (natriuresis and vasodilation), so we measure it as a stretch gauge. A low BNP in acute dyspnoea essentially rules heart failure out as the cause.

28 Renal Function, GFR, and Acute Kidney Injury ▶
Key idea — Glomerular filtration is a hydraulic event, not a chemical one. Anything that lowers the pressure inside the glomerular capillary lowers GFR, and because surviving nephrons hyperfilter to cover for dead ones, serum creatinine stays deceptively normal until roughly half the filtering capacity is already gone.

Where GFR actually comes from

Each kidney holds about a million nephrons, and the glomerulus is the one capillary bed in the body sandwiched between two arterioles rather than between an arteriole and a venule. That arrangement is the whole trick: the kidney can tune inflow and outflow independently. Net filtration pressure is glomerular hydrostatic pressure (about 55 mmHg) minus capsular hydrostatic pressure (about 15) minus glomerular oncotic pressure (about 30), leaving roughly 10 mmHg of usable driving force and a normal GFR near 125 mL/min. To defend that number when perfusion drops, the kidney dilates the afferent arteriole using locally made prostaglandins and constricts the efferent arteriole using angiotensin II, effectively damming the blood inside the tuft.

This is why two very common drugs become dangerous in a dehydrated patient. NSAIDs block prostaglandin synthesis, so the afferent arteriole cannot dilate; ACE inhibitors and ARBs block angiotensin II, so the efferent arteriole cannot constrict. Either alone is usually tolerated. Together, in someone who is already volume down, both autoregulatory arms are amputated and glomerular pressure collapses. Downstream, the proximal tubule reclaims about 65 percent of the filtrate plus all the glucose, the loop of Henle builds the medullary concentration gradient (the target of the potent loop diuretics), the distal tubule fine-tunes sodium and calcium, and the collecting duct is the hormone-controlled final say, where ADH inserts aquaporins and aldosterone drives the sodium-potassium exchange.

AKI is defined as a creatinine rise of at least 0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, or 1.5 times baseline within 7 days, or urine output under 0.5 mL/kg/hr for 6 hours. Prerenal is a perfusion problem with structurally intact, starving tubules, so those tubules hoard sodium and water furiously. Intrarenal, most often acute tubular necrosis, means the tubular cells are dead or dying, so they can neither reclaim sodium nor build a concentration gradient. Postrenal is a plumbing blockage that transmits back-pressure up to the glomerulus and cancels the filtration gradient.

Acute tubular necrosis runs a predictable three-act course, and each act has its own danger. The oliguric phase lasts one to three weeks and is where patients die: potassium, acid and fluid accumulate with nowhere to go. The diuretic phase is deceptive good news, because filtration recovers before reabsorption does, so the patient pours out litres of dilute urine and can crash into hypovolaemia and hypokalaemia while everyone is celebrating. 29 Glomerular Disease and Chronic Kidney Disease ▶
Key idea — Glomerular disease splits on what the injury does to the filter. Inflammation that plugs and tears capillary walls lets red cells escape and drops GFR, producing nephritic syndrome. Loss of the charge and size barrier without inflammation lets albumin pour out through a filter that is still wide open, producing nephrotic syndrome. Every downstream finding, the oedema pattern, the blood pressure, the lipids, the clots, follows from that one distinction.

Nephritic versus nephrotic

The filter has three layers: a fenestrated endothelium, a glomerular basement membrane studded with negatively charged heparan sulphate, and podocyte foot processes joined by slit diaphragms made of nephrin. In nephritic disease, immune complexes lodge in the capillary wall, complement is fixed, and inflammatory cells proliferate into the tuft. The wall becomes leaky enough for red cells to squeeze through but the lumen also narrows, so filtration falls, sodium and water are retained, and blood pressure rises. In nephrotic disease there is no inflammatory infiltrate at all; the podocytes simply efface and the negative charge barrier is lost, so albumin, which is small and negatively charged and normally repelled, streams into the urine at more than 3.5 grams a day.

Pearl — A leaky glomerulus is not selective about which protein it loses; it loses everything near albumin's size. That single fact explains the whole nephrotic complication list. Immunoglobulin G washes out, so encapsulated organisms cause peritonitis and pneumonia. Meanwhile the liver, sensing collapsing oncotic pressure, ramps up synthesis of all proteins indiscriminately, including lipoproteins, which is why cholesterol soars and fat appears in the urine.

Chronic kidney disease: what dies when nephrons die

CKD means a GFR under 60 mL/min for three months or more, or persistent markers of damage such as albuminuria, and it is staged G1 through G5 with dialysis usually considered near a GFR of 15. Diabetes and hypertension together account for roughly two-thirds of cases. The engine of progression is hyperfiltration: as nephrons drop out, the survivors hypertrophy and filter harder to hold total GFR steady, which is exactly why creatinine looks normal for so long, and exactly why the disease is self-perpetuating. That elevated pressure inside the surviving tufts sclerose them in turn. This is the entire rationale for ACE inhibitors, ARBs and SGLT2 inhibitors in CKD: they lower intraglomerular pressure, accepting a small immediate dip in GFR in exchange for preserving nephrons over years.

30 Respiratory: Obstruction, Restriction, Hypoxaemia, and the Acute Failures ▶
Key idea — Every respiratory problem answers two questions. First, is it hard to get air out (obstructive, air trapping, low FEV1/FVC) or hard to get air in (restrictive, small stiff lungs, preserved ratio)? Second, is the oxygenation failure one of ventilation failing to reach blood (shunt) or blood failing to reach ventilation (dead space)? Fix those two axes and everything else is detail.

Obstructive versus restrictive

Spirometry answers the first question mechanically. In obstruction the airways narrow or collapse during exhalation, so forced expiratory volume in one second falls far more than forced vital capacity does and the ratio drops below 0.70; the air that cannot get out stays behind, so residual volume and total lung capacity rise and the chest becomes hyperinflated. In restriction the lung or chest wall simply will not expand, so every volume shrinks together, total lung capacity falls, and the ratio stays normal or even climbs above 0.80 because a stiff lung empties briskly. Diffusing capacity then subdivides the causes: it is low in emphysema (alveolar surface destroyed) and in fibrosis (membrane thickened), but normal or high in asthma and in purely extrapulmonary restriction such as obesity or kyphoscoliosis.

Tension pneumothorax

A tension pneumothorax is a one-way flap valve. Air enters the pleural space on inspiration and cannot escape on expiration, so intrapleural pressure climbs above atmospheric and rises with every single breath. The lung on that side collapses first, but the lethal step comes next: the mediastinum is pushed toward the opposite hemithorax, and the shift kinks the superior and inferior vena cava. Venous return falls, the right ventricle has nothing to pump, and cardiac output collapses. It is worth being precise about this, because it changes the urgency: the patient is dying of obstructive shock, of absent preload, not primarily of failed oxygenation.

Red flagTension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis and must never wait for a chest film. Look for sudden severe dyspnoea and pleuritic pain, absent breath sounds with hyperresonance on the affected side, tracheal deviation away from that side, distended neck veins, hypotension and tachycardia. Treat immediately with needle decompression, passing the needle over the top of the rib in the second intercostal space at the midclavicular line or the fifth space in the anterior axillary line, then place a chest tube. In a mechanically ventilated patient, a sudden spike in peak airway pressure together with hypotension is a tension pneumothorax until proven otherwise.
One-way flap → pleural pressure rises with each breath → lung collapses → mediastinum shifts → vena cava kinks → preload falls → obstructive shock and arrest

Q1. Two days after severe pancreatitis a patient develops bilateral infiltrates, PaO2 52 mmHg on FiO2 0.8 (PaO2/FiO2 of 65), a normal jugular venous pressure, BNP of 40 pg/mL and a normal ejection fraction on echo. Why does increasing the FiO2 barely change his PaO2?
