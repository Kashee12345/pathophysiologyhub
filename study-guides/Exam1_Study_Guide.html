TOPIC 1 💦 Fluid Compartments & Pressures MUST KNOW▼

Where Body Water Lives

Compartment% of Body FluidDominant Electrolyte
Intracellular (ICF)~2/3 (~67%)High Potassium (K⁺)
Extracellular (ECF)~1/3 (~33%)High Sodium (Na⁺)

The ECF further breaks down into:

  • Intravascular fluid — inside blood vessels (plasma).
  • Interstitial fluid — in tissue spaces around cells.
  • Transcellular fluid — smaller spaces (CSF, joint fluid, peritoneal, etc.).

Hydrostatic vs. Oncotic Pressure

👉 HYDROSTATIC pressure
A "pushing" force generated by the blood inside vessels. Pushes fluid OUT of capillaries into tissues.

What raises it? High BP, heart failure, IV fluid overload, blocked venous return.		 👈 ONCOTIC pressure
A "pulling" force created mainly by albumin (a plasma protein). Pulls fluid INTO the vessel.

What lowers it? Liver failure (less albumin made), malnutrition (no protein in), kidney disease (albumin lost in urine).

Causes of Peripheral Edema (fluid in tissues)

Edema = fluid escapes from vessels into tissue. Happens when:

  • Hydrostatic pressure goes UP → pushed too hard out (heart failure, hypertension).
  • Oncotic pressure goes DOWN → nothing pulling fluid back in (liver failure, malnutrition, kidney disease losing albumin).
  • Lymphatic blockage → can't drain fluid back (lymphedema, post-mastectomy).
  • Increased capillary permeability → vessel walls leaky (inflammation, sepsis, burns).
💡 Memory aid: Hydrostatic = "Hose pushing out" · Oncotic = "Owe me back" (albumin pulling). Edema = pushers win over pullers.
⚠️ Common confusion: Sodium is the dominant electrolyte outside the cell (ECF). Potassium is dominant inside (ICF). Lots of students mix these up.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What % of body fluid is intracellular vs extracellular, and which electrolyte dominates each?
    Show answer
    ICF ~2/3 (high K⁺). ECF ~1/3 (high Na⁺).
  2. Compare hydrostatic and oncotic pressure.
    Show answer
    Hydrostatic = pushing force, pushes fluid OUT of vessels. Oncotic = pulling force from albumin, pulls fluid INTO vessels.
  3. List 4 causes of peripheral edema.
    Show answer
    Increased hydrostatic pressure (heart failure), decreased oncotic pressure (low albumin from liver failure / malnutrition / kidney disease), lymphatic obstruction, increased capillary permeability (inflammation/sepsis).
  4. What's the difference between intravascular and interstitial fluid?
    Show answer
    Intravascular = inside blood vessels (plasma). Interstitial = around tissue cells, outside vessels.
  5. Why does a patient with cirrhosis develop edema and ascites?
    Show answer
    The damaged liver produces less albumin → low oncotic pressure → fluid stays in tissues/abdomen instead of being pulled back into vessels.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. The dominant electrolyte inside cells is:
    A) Sodium · B) Potassium · C) Calcium · D) Chloride
    Show answer
    B. Potassium dominates ICF.
  2. A patient with severe burns develops generalized edema. The most likely cause is:
    A) Lymphatic blockage · B) Increased capillary permeability · C) Low hydrostatic pressure · D) High oncotic pressure
    Show answer
    B. Burn injury makes capillaries leaky — fluid escapes into tissues.
  3. Which protein is primarily responsible for oncotic pressure?
    A) Hemoglobin · B) Globulin · C) Albumin · D) Fibrinogen
    Show answer
    C. Albumin.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A 68-year-old with congestive heart failure presents with 3+ pitting edema in both ankles and difficulty breathing. Lab shows normal albumin. Explain the mechanism of the edema and why the lung symptoms developed.
Show answer
Heart failure → blood backs up in the venous system → increased hydrostatic pressure in capillaries → fluid pushed out into tissues (peripheral edema). When backup is in the left heart, pulmonary capillary pressure rises and fluid leaks into alveoli → pulmonary edema (shortness of breath, crackles). Albumin is normal because the cause is hydrostatic, not oncotic.

  • I can identify albumin's role in fluid balance.

TOPIC 2 🧡 Fluid Regulation Hormones — RAAS, ADH, ANP/BNP MUST KNOW▼

The RAAS System (Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System)

What activates it: low blood pressure, low blood volume, or low sodium detected by the kidneys.

The cascade:

  1. Kidneys release renin in response to low BP/volume.
  2. Renin converts angiotensinogen (from liver) into angiotensin I.
  3. ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) in the lungs converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II.
  4. Angiotensin II does 2 main things:
    • Vasoconstricts blood vessels → raises BP.
    • Triggers the adrenal cortex to release aldosterone.

Aldosterone vs. ADH — Effects on Kidneys

HormoneFromKidney EffectNet Effect
AldosteroneAdrenal cortexReabsorbs Na⁺ (and water with it); excretes K⁺↑ BP, ↑ volume; ↓ K⁺
ADH (Vasopressin)Posterior pituitaryReabsorbs water (no sodium)↑ BP, ↑ volume; concentrates urine

ANP & BNP — The "Off-Switch"

ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide) is released by the atria of the heart; BNP (brain/B-type natriuretic peptide) is released by the ventricles. Both are released when the heart is stretched (high volume or high pressure).

Net effects: opposite of RAAS!

  • Excrete sodium (natriuresis) and water.
  • Dilate blood vessels.
  • Decrease BP and volume.
  • Inhibit renin and aldosterone.

Clinical use: elevated BNP is a marker of heart failure.

Angiotensin II — Main Actions

  • Vasoconstriction → raises BP.
  • Stimulates aldosterone release.
  • Stimulates ADH release.
  • Triggers thirst.

This is why ACE inhibitors ("–pril" drugs like lisinopril) are used to lower BP — they block this whole cascade.

💡 Memory aids:
  • RAAS = "Raises pressure." Low BP → activates RAAS → saves Na & water → BP goes up.
  • ANP/BNP = "Anti-pressure." High pressure stretches the heart → ANP/BNP → dumps Na & water → BP goes down.
  • Aldosterone = "Aldo loves sodium."
  • ADH = "Anti-Diuretic" → holds onto water.
⚠️ Common confusion: Aldosterone keeps Na+water; ADH keeps ONLY water (no Na). So in conditions with SIADH (too much ADH), serum Na drops (dilutional hyponatremia).

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. What activates the RAAS system?
    Show answer
    Low BP, low blood volume, or low sodium detected by the kidneys.
  2. List the 4 main actions of angiotensin II.
    Show answer
    Vasoconstriction; triggers aldosterone; triggers ADH; stimulates thirst.
  3. Compare what aldosterone and ADH do at the kidney.
    Show answer
    Aldosterone reabsorbs sodium (and water with it) and excretes potassium. ADH reabsorbs water only (no sodium).
  4. What are the net effects of ANP and BNP?
    Show answer
    Excrete sodium and water, dilate vessels, decrease BP and volume, inhibit RAAS. Opposite of RAAS.
  5. Why is BNP elevated in heart failure?
    Show answer
    Failing ventricles are overstretched by backed-up fluid, which triggers BNP release. Higher BNP = more stretch = more severe failure.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Aldosterone causes the kidneys to:
    A) Excrete sodium · B) Reabsorb sodium and water; excrete potassium · C) Reabsorb water only · D) Dilate vessels
    Show answer
    B. Reabsorbs Na (and water) & excretes K.
  2. An elevated BNP level most directly suggests:
    A) Liver failure · B) Dehydration · C) Heart failure · D) Diabetes
    Show answer
    C. Heart failure (ventricular stretch).
  3. Which hormone primarily controls water reabsorption WITHOUT affecting sodium?
    A) Aldosterone · B) ADH · C) Angiotensin II · D) Renin
    Show answer
    B. ADH = water only.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A trauma patient has lost a significant amount of blood. The body's compensatory response includes which RAAS-related steps, and what would you expect to see in vital signs and urine output before treatment?
Show answer
Low volume → kidneys release renin → angiotensin II → vasoconstriction (BP rises a bit; HR rises) and aldosterone + ADH release (kidneys hold onto Na and water). Expected findings: tachycardia, narrow pulse pressure, decreased urine output (oliguria), and concentrated urine. Fluid resuscitation reverses the cascade.

  • I can contrast aldosterone (Na + water + K excretion) with ADH (water only).
  • I can describe the opposite role of ANP/BNP.
  • I can explain why BNP is a heart failure marker.

TOPIC 3 🧪 Electrolyte Imbalances (Na, K, Ca, P, Mg) MUST KNOW▼

Hint: Water follows sodium. Potassium is "the cardiac one." Phosphate and magnesium move opposite to calcium.

⚒ Sodium (Na⁺): 135–145 mEq/L

Function: fluid balance (kidneys control it).

Hyponatremia (<135)Hypernatremia (>145)
CausesSIADH, diuretics, vomiting/diarrhea, water overload, heart failure (dilutional)Dehydration, diabetes insipidus, excessive Na intake, burns
S/SxHeadache, confusion, lethargy, seizures, muscle cramps. Brain swells (cells take in water).Thirst, dry mucous membranes, irritability, lethargy, seizures. Cells shrink.

❤️ Potassium (K⁺): 3.5–5.0 mEq/L — "the cardiac one"

Function: cardiac contractility & acid-base regulation.

Hypokalemia (<3.5)Hyperkalemia (>5.0)
CausesDiuretics (esp. loop), vomiting/diarrhea, alkalosis, low intake, NG suctionKidney failure, acidosis, K-sparing diuretics, tissue damage (burns/crush), Addison's
S/SxMuscle weakness, fatigue, leg cramps, constipation, flat T waves & U waves on EKG, dysrhythmiasMuscle weakness, paresthesias, peaked T waves on EKG, dysrhythmias, cardiac arrest

🧹 Calcium (Ca): 9.0–10.5 mg/dL

Function: muscle contraction, neurotransmitter release, clotting, bone strength.

Hypocalcemia (<9.0)Hypercalcemia (>10.5)
CausesHypoparathyroidism, vitamin D deficiency, kidney disease, malabsorption, pancreatitisHyperparathyroidism, cancer (bone metastases), excessive vitamin D, immobility
S/SxMuscle twitching, tetany, Trousseau's sign (carpal spasm), Chvostek's sign (facial twitch), paresthesias, seizuresMuscle weakness, bone pain, kidney stones, constipation, lethargy. "Bones, stones, groans, psychic moans."

🧸 Phosphate (P): 2.5–4.5 mg/dL — opposite of calcium

Function: membrane stability, bone & teeth, cell energy, DNA/RNA.

Hypophosphatemia (<2.5)Hyperphosphatemia (>4.5)
CausesAlcoholism, burns, refeeding syndrome, malabsorptionKidney failure (most common)
S/SxMuscle pain & weakness, irritability, constipation, confusionMuscle cramps, bone pain, pruritus (itching); s/sx mirror hypocalcemia (Ca drops as P rises)

🧋 Magnesium (Mg): 1.3–2.1 mEq/L — "the muscle relaxer"

Function: muscle relaxant, potassium regulation. Acts opposite of calcium.

Hypomagnesemia (<1.3)Hypermagnesemia (>2.1)
CausesAlcoholism, malabsorption, diarrhea, diureticsKidney failure, antacids/laxatives with Mg, IV Mg in preeclampsia
S/SxLooks like hypocalcemia — tetany, twitching, Trousseau's/Chvostek's, dysrhythmias, seizuresLethargy, hypotension, depressed deep tendon reflexes, respiratory depression, cardiac arrest
💡 Master pattern:
  • Calcium ↔ Phosphate: one up, the other down.
  • Calcium ↔ Magnesium: function-wise opposite, but their lab values often move together (both deficient in alcoholism, both excessive in kidney failure).
  • Hypocalcemia s/sx = Hypomagnesemia s/sx: tetany, Trousseau's, Chvostek's.
  • EKG changes: hyperkalemia = peaked T; hypokalemia = flat T & U waves.
⚠️ Common confusion: "Hypo" and "Hyper" can cause the SAME symptom (e.g., muscle weakness in BOTH hyperkalemia and hypokalemia). Pin down the lab value and the specific signs (peaked vs flat T waves) to differentiate.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. State the normal lab range for Na, K, Ca, P, and Mg.
    Show answer
    Na: 135–145 mEq/L. K: 3.5–5.0 mEq/L. Ca: 9.0–10.5 mg/dL. P: 2.5–4.5 mg/dL. Mg: 1.3–2.1 mEq/L.
  2. What EKG change classically appears in hyperkalemia vs hypokalemia?
    Show answer
    Hyperkalemia: peaked T waves. Hypokalemia: flattened T waves with prominent U waves.
  3. Name Trousseau's and Chvostek's signs and what electrolyte imbalance they indicate.
    Show answer
    Trousseau's = carpal spasm after BP cuff is inflated. Chvostek's = facial twitch when cheek is tapped. Both indicate hypocalcemia (or hypomagnesemia).
  4. Why does kidney failure tend to cause hyperphosphatemia AND hypocalcemia?
    Show answer
    Failing kidneys can't excrete phosphate → P builds up. Calcium and phosphate move in opposite directions, so Ca drops as P rises. Failed kidneys also activate less vitamin D, so Ca absorption falls further.
  5. What are signs of magnesium toxicity (hypermagnesemia)?
    Show answer
    Depressed deep tendon reflexes, lethargy, hypotension, respiratory depression, cardiac arrest. (Why nurses check reflexes during IV Mg for preeclampsia.)

✅ Multiple Choice Practice (SATA practice too)

  1. Which lab finding is most consistent with hyperkalemia?
    A) Flat T waves · B) Peaked T waves · C) Prolonged QT · D) U waves
    Show answer
    B. Peaked T waves.
  2. Select ALL that apply — causes of hypokalemia:
    A) Loop diuretics · B) Vomiting · C) Kidney failure · D) Alkalosis · E) Acidosis
    Show answer
    A, B, D. Loop diuretics waste K, vomiting loses K, and alkalosis shifts K into cells (low serum K). Kidney failure and acidosis cause hyper-K, not hypo.
  3. A patient with hypocalcemia is likely to show:
    A) Bone pain · B) Constipation · C) Trousseau's sign · D) Lethargy
    Show answer
    C. Trousseau's sign — carpal spasm with BP cuff inflation.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with chronic kidney disease has the following labs: K⁺ 6.1, Ca 7.8, P 6.5. The patient feels weak and reports tingling around the mouth. Which two abnormal labs are likely connected, and what does the EKG most likely show?
Show answer
Ca (7.8 = low) and P (6.5 = high) are connected: failing kidneys can't excrete P, and Ca/P move opposite. Tingling = hypocalcemia. K (6.1 = high) is from inability to excrete K. EKG would show peaked T waves. Treatment may include calcium gluconate (stabilizes the heart) and measures to lower K (insulin/D50, kayexalate, dialysis).

  • I can recite the normal ranges for Na, K, Ca, P, Mg from memory.
  • I can list at least 3 causes and 3 s/sx for each high and low electrolyte.
  • I can identify the EKG findings for hypo- and hyperkalemia.
  • I can describe Trousseau's and Chvostek's signs.
  • I can explain why kidney failure links high P, low Ca, and high K.

TOPIC 4 ⚖ Acid-Base Foundations & Buffer Systems MUST KNOW▼

Acid-Base Basics

  • Normal metabolism creates CO₂ and H⁺ — both are acids.
  • Body's goal: keep pH between 7.35–7.45 (very narrow range).
  • pH and H⁺ are inversely proportional: if H⁺ is high, pH is low (acidic).
  • Acids: CO₂, H⁺. Base: HCO₃⁻.

The 3 Buffer Systems

BufferSpeedHow It Works
Serum (chemical)Immediate (seconds)Proteins bind H⁺; K⁺ exchanges with H⁺ across cell membranes
RespiratoryMinutesLungs adjust ventilation. ↑ respiration blows off CO₂ → pH rises. ↓ respiration retains CO₂ → pH falls. Kussmaul = deep, rapid breaths (DKA)
Renal2–3 days — STRONGESTKidneys excrete H⁺ and reabsorb/produce HCO₃⁻

Why K⁺ Shifts With pH

In acidosis, H⁺ floods into cells (to buffer) and K⁺ gets pushed out into the blood → acidosis = hyperkalemia. The reverse in alkalosis → alkalosis = hypokalemia.

💡 Memory aids:
  • CO₂ = ACID. High CO₂ → acidic.
  • HCO₃⁻ = BASE. High HCO₃⁻ → alkaline.
  • "Acidosis = K up." "Alkalosis = K down."
⚠️ Common confusion: Lower pH = MORE acid, not less. The smaller number means more H⁺.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. State all 4 normal ABG values.
    Show answer
    pH 7.35–7.45 · PaCO₂ 35–45 mmHg · HCO₃⁻ 22–26 mEq/L · PaO₂ 80–100 mmHg.
  2. Which is acid and which is base: CO₂ or HCO₃⁻?
    Show answer
    CO₂ = acid. HCO₃⁻ = base.
  3. Rank the 3 buffer systems by speed.
    Show answer
    Serum (seconds) → Respiratory (minutes) → Renal (days, strongest).
  4. What is Kussmaul breathing?
    Show answer
    Deep, rapid breathing that blows off CO₂ to compensate for metabolic acidosis (classic in DKA).
  5. How does acidosis affect serum K⁺?
    Show answer
    Causes hyperkalemia — H⁺ moves into cells, K⁺ pushed out.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Which buffer is fastest?
    A) Serum · B) Respiratory · C) Renal · D) Lymphatic
    Show answer
    A. Serum (chemical) — works in seconds.
  2. The strongest buffer system is:
    A) Serum · B) Respiratory · C) Renal · D) Cardiac
    Show answer
    C. Renal — slowest but strongest.
  3. pH 7.30 + PaCO₂ 50 indicates:
    A) Metabolic acidosis · B) Respiratory acidosis · C) Metabolic alkalosis · D) Respiratory alkalosis
    Show answer
    B. Acidic pH + high CO₂ = respiratory acidosis.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A DKA patient is breathing fast and deep. Which buffer system is engaged and why?
Show answer
DKA causes metabolic acidosis (ketones). The respiratory buffer hyperventilates to blow off CO₂ (acid) and raise pH. Deep rapid breathing = Kussmaul respirations.

TOPIC 5 🧪 Acidosis & Alkalosis Causes MUST KNOW▼

Acidosis (pH < 7.35)

TypeCausesKey Findings
Metabolic acidosisDKA (ketones) · lactic acidosis · liver/kidney failure · severe diarrhea (loses HCO₃⁻)High anion gap · hyperkalemia · Kussmaul compensation
Respiratory acidosisHypoventilation: COPD, pneumonia, opioid overdose, sedationHypercapnia (↑CO₂) · hyperkalemia

Alkalosis (pH > 7.45)

TypeCausesKey Findings
Metabolic alkalosisSevere vomiting · NG suction (lose H⁺) · excess calcium carbonate (antacids)Hypokalemia
Respiratory alkalosisHyperventilation: panic, anxiety, fever, pain, mechanical over-ventilationHypocapnia (↓CO₂) · hypokalemia

Consequences of Acidosis on the Body

  • Neuro: confusion, lethargy, coma.
  • Cardiac: dysrhythmias, ↓contractility, vasodilation, hypotension.
  • Respiratory: Kussmaul breathing (compensation).
  • Bone: chronic acidosis pulls Ca out of bone.
  • Electrolytes: hyperkalemia.
  • Enzymes: denature at extreme pH.
💡 Quick patterns:
  • Vomiting/NG suction = ALKALOSIS (lose gastric acid).
  • Diarrhea = ACIDOSIS (lose intestinal HCO₃⁻).
  • DKA = METABOLIC ACIDOSIS.
  • COPD/opioid = RESPIRATORY ACIDOSIS.
  • Panic attack = RESPIRATORY ALKALOSIS.
⚠️ Common confusion: Direction of GI loss matters. Upper GI (vomiting) loses acid = alkalosis. Lower GI (diarrhea) loses base = acidosis.

❓ Free-Response Practice

  1. List 2 causes of metabolic acidosis and 2 of respiratory acidosis.
    Show answer
    Metabolic: DKA, severe diarrhea. Respiratory: COPD, opioid overdose.
  2. List 2 causes of metabolic alkalosis and 2 of respiratory alkalosis.
    Show answer
    Metabolic: severe vomiting, NG suction. Respiratory: panic attack, fever-induced hyperventilation.
  3. What K⁺ pattern do you expect in each acid-base disorder?
    Show answer
    Acidosis → hyperkalemia. Alkalosis → hypokalemia.
  4. List 3 consequences of acidosis on the body.
    Show answer
    Confusion/coma, dysrhythmias, hyperkalemia, Kussmaul compensation, bone Ca loss (chronic).
  5. What does a high anion gap indicate?
    Show answer
    Metabolic acidosis with accumulated unmeasured acid — DKA, lactic acidosis, salicylates, renal failure.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. Severe vomiting most likely causes:
    A) Metabolic acidosis · B) Metabolic alkalosis · C) Respiratory acidosis · D) Respiratory alkalosis
    Show answer
    B. Loss of HCl from stomach → metabolic alkalosis.
  2. An opioid overdose patient is at greatest risk for:
    A) Metabolic acidosis · B) Metabolic alkalosis · C) Respiratory acidosis · D) Respiratory alkalosis
    Show answer
    C. Hypoventilation → CO₂ retention.
  3. Severe diarrhea most likely causes:
    A) Metabolic acidosis · B) Metabolic alkalosis · C) Respiratory acidosis · D) Respiratory alkalosis
    Show answer
    A. Lose HCO₃⁻ from intestines → metabolic acidosis.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A COPD patient comes in with worsening dyspnea. ABG: pH 7.30, PaCO₂ 60, HCO₃⁻ 30. Identify the disorder, the compensation effort, and expected K⁺.
Show answer
Acidic pH; high CO₂ (matches); high HCO₃⁻ (kidney compensation). Respiratory acidosis with partial metabolic compensation. Expect hyperkalemia. Treat the underlying cause — improve ventilation, bronchodilators, careful O₂.

TOPIC 6 🧬 ABG Interpretation — Step-by-Step with Worked Cases MUST KNOW▼

Why we draw ABGs

To assess oxygenation (PaO₂), ventilation (PaCO₂), and acid-base balance — especially critical for ICU, vent management, and metabolic emergencies.

3-Step ABG Method

  1. Look at pH: <7.35 = acidosis. >7.45 = alkalosis. 7.35–7.45 = normal (may be compensated).
  2. Decide respiratory or metabolic:
    • Check PaCO₂ (respiratory). >45 = acidic. <35 = alkalotic.
    • Check HCO₃⁻ (metabolic). <22 = acidic. >26 = alkalotic.
    • The value matching the pH direction is the CAUSE.
  3. Check for compensation: if the OTHER value is also abnormal but moving OPPOSITE the pH, the body is compensating.

ROME Memory Trick

ROMEMeaning
RespiratoryOpposite — pH and CO₂ go OPPOSITE directions
MetabolicEqual — pH and HCO₃⁻ go SAME direction

Compensation Levels

  • Uncompensated — only the causative value is abnormal.
  • Partially compensated — both values abnormal; pH still out of range.
  • Fully compensated — both values abnormal; pH back in normal range.

Worked Examples

Case 1: pH 7.29 · CO₂ 47 · HCO₃⁻ 24

Step 1: pH 7.29 → acidic.

Step 2: CO₂ 47 = high (acidic). HCO₃⁻ 24 = normal. CO₂ matches pH → respiratory cause.

Step 3: HCO₃⁻ is normal → no compensation yet.

Diagnosis: Uncompensated Respiratory Acidosis. Think: hypoventilation, opioid OD, acute COPD flare.

Case 2: pH 7.25 · CO₂ 49 · HCO₃⁻ 30

Step 1: pH 7.25 → acidic.

Step 2: CO₂ 49 = high (acidic). HCO₃⁻ 30 = high (alkaline). CO₂ matches pH → respiratory.

Step 3: HCO₃⁻ is high — kidneys trying to compensate. pH still <7.35.

Diagnosis: Respiratory Acidosis with Partial Metabolic Compensation. Classic chronic COPD picture.

Case 3: pH 7.31 · CO₂ 30 · HCO₃⁻ 18

Step 1: pH 7.31 → acidic.

Step 2: CO₂ 30 = low (alkaline). HCO₃⁻ 18 = low (acidic). HCO₃⁻ matches pH → metabolic cause.

Step 3: CO₂ is low — lungs hyperventilating to compensate. pH still acidic.

Diagnosis: Metabolic Acidosis with Partial Respiratory Compensation. Classic DKA — Kussmaul breathing.

Case 4: pH 7.48 · CO₂ 41 · HCO₃⁻ 29

Step 1: pH 7.48 → alkaline.

Step 2: CO₂ 41 = normal. HCO₃⁻ 29 = high (alkaline). HCO₃⁻ matches pH → metabolic.

Step 3: CO₂ normal → no respiratory compensation yet.

Diagnosis: Uncompensated Metabolic Alkalosis. Think: severe vomiting, NG suction, antacid overuse.

💡 Tic-tac-toe shortcut: Make 3 columns — ACID / NORMAL / BASE. Drop each value (pH, CO₂, HCO₃⁻) in the right column. Two in ACID = acidosis. The value alongside pH tells you the type. Remember: CO₂ goes opposite to its column intuition — high CO₂ is ACID, low CO₂ is BASE.
⚠️ Common confusion: "Compensation" never overshoots back to normal in a single direction. If pH is normal AND both CO₂ and HCO₃⁻ are abnormal, it's fully compensated — the original cause is wherever pH is closer to (just barely) crossed the line.

Testing for Microbes

  • Gram stain (bacteria):
    • Gram-positive — thick cell wall, stains PURPLE.
    • Gram-negative — thin cell wall, stains PINK/RED. (Harder to treat — outer membrane is a barrier.)
  • Culture & Sensitivity — Culture identifies the organism; sensitivity shows which antibiotic will work.

Antibiotic Resistance — Contributing Factors

  • Inappropriate antibiotic use (prescribed for viral infections like the cold/flu).
  • Not finishing the full course of antibiotics — surviving bacteria multiply with resistance.
  • Widespread use in agriculture & livestock.
  • Patient non-adherence.
  • Overuse of broad-spectrum antibiotics.
  • Hospital-acquired exposure (MRSA, VRE, C. diff).
💡 Gram-stain trick: Positive = Purple. (Negative stains pink/red — the opposite of purple.) Gram-negatives tend to be harder to treat because their outer membrane keeps drugs out.
⚠️ Common confusion: Antibiotics work ONLY on bacteria — not viruses. Giving antibiotics for a cold or flu drives resistance and doesn't help the patient.

TOPIC 8 🫕 Stages of Infection, Transmission, & TB MUST KNOW▼

Routes of Transmission

  • Direct contact — person to person (touch, sex). Examples: HIV, herpes, scabies.
  • Indirect contact — fomites (contaminated objects). Example: doorknobs, IV poles.
  • Droplet — large respiratory droplets (within ~3–6 feet). Examples: influenza, pertussis, COVID.
  • Airborne — small particles travel long distances. Examples: TB, measles, varicella.
  • Vector-borne — insects/animals. Examples: malaria (mosquito), Lyme (tick).
  • Vehicle/foodborne — contaminated food or water.
  • Vertical (mother to baby) — placenta, breast milk, birth canal.

Stages of Infection

StageWhat's happeningS/Sx
1. ExposurePathogen enters bodyNone yet
2. IncubationPathogen multiplies; person feels fineUsually asymptomatic, but may be contagious
3. ProdromeEarly non-specific symptomsFatigue, low-grade fever, malaise; HIGHLY contagious
4. Acute (full)Peak symptoms, immune response at maxSpecific s/sx of the infection (rash, cough, etc.)
5. ConvalescentBody clears the pathogen, healingSymptoms gradually resolve
6. ResolutionRecovery complete or chronic state establishedNo symptoms, or symptoms persist long-term

Tuberculosis (TB)

Bacterial infection by Mycobacterium tuberculosis; usually lives in the lungs. Airborne transmission.

Latent TBActive TB
Immune system has walled off the bacteria. No symptoms. Not contagious. Positive skin test (PPD/TST) but normal chest X-ray. Person is well.Immune system weakened — TB escapes the wall. Contagious. Hemoptysis (coughing up blood), chest pain, productive cough > 3 weeks, fever, chills, weakness, fatigue, night sweats, weight loss.
💡 Stages mnemonic: "Every Infection Provides Acute Care Recovery" — Exposure, Incubation, Prodrome, Acute, Convalescent, Resolution.
⚠️ Common confusion: Latent TB = positive test but NO symptoms, NOT contagious. Active TB = symptoms AND contagious. Both need treatment, but the regimens differ.

The 4 Types of Hypersensitivity Reactions

An over-reaction of the immune system to an antigen — the body's response damages tissue more than the antigen itself.

TypeKey FeatureExamples
Type I — "Allergic"IgE on mast cells → histamine release. Immediate (mins).Anaphylaxis, hay fever, asthma, food allergies, latex allergy

Anaphylaxis — A Type I Emergency

Massive IgE-mediated histamine release leading to:

  • Skin: hives, flushing, swelling (angioedema).
  • Respiratory: wheezing, stridor, throat tightening, airway compromise.
  • Cardiovascular: hypotension (distributive shock), tachycardia.
  • GI: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea.

Treatment: IM epinephrine (first-line), then oxygen, IV fluids, antihistamines, steroids, airway management.

TOPIC 12 🧴 HIV/AIDS, Immunodeficiency, Autoimmunity & Blood Cancers MUST KNOW▼

HIV & AIDS

HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus) is a retrovirus that targets CD4 T helper cells — the "conductor" of the immune response.

  • As CD4 count drops, the immune system loses coordination.
  • AIDS is the advanced stage: CD4 count < 200 OR an AIDS-defining opportunistic infection.

Opportunistic Infections in AIDS

SystemExamples
RespiratoryTB, PCP (Pneumocystis pneumonia)
GIOral thrush (Candida), esophageal candidiasis
CNSToxoplasmosis, cryptococcal meningitis
CancersKaposi sarcoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma

Immunodeficiency — Causes

  • Cancer and cancer treatment (chemo, radiation).
  • HIV infection.
  • Diabetes (impaired neutrophil function, high glucose feeds organisms).
  • Stress (chronic cortisol suppresses immunity).
  • Malnutrition.
  • Splenectomy (lose key immune organ).
  • Immunosuppressive drugs (transplant, autoimmune disease).
  • Age extremes (very young or very old).

Leukemia (Cancer of WBCs)

Leukemia = cancer of early blood cells. Abnormal proliferation; cells don't function.

  • Lymphoblastic leukemias → affect cells that become lymphocytes (B and T).
  • Myelocytic leukemias → affect cells that become basophils, eosinophils, neutrophils.
  • Acute = rapid onset, immature cells, more aggressive.
  • Chronic = slower, more mature cells, longer course.

Common features across leukemias: fatigue, anemia, infections, bleeding/bruising, weight loss, splenomegaly.

    A) Common cold · B) Kaposi sarcoma · C) PCP pneumonia · D) Oral thrush · E) Strep throat
    Show answer
    B, C. Kaposi sarcoma and PCP are AIDS-defining. Oral thrush is common in immunosuppression but not AIDS-defining alone.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A 35-year-old with untreated HIV presents with shortness of breath, a CD4 count of 95, and chest X-ray showing diffuse infiltrates. Sputum confirms Pneumocystis jirovecii. Why has this patient developed PCP, and what is their diagnosis now?
Show answer
CD4 count below 200 makes the patient unable to fight off opportunistic infections like Pneumocystis (PCP). The combination of CD4 < 200 plus an AIDS-defining opportunistic infection (PCP) means the patient has progressed from HIV to AIDS. Treatment: start antiretroviral therapy + treat PCP (TMP-SMX) + prophylaxis going forward.

📚 Quick Glossary

Fluids & Hormones
  • ICF / ECF — intracellular (high K⁺) / extracellular (high Na⁺).
  • Hydrostatic pressure — pushing force from blood.
  • Oncotic pressure — pulling force from albumin.
  • Edema — fluid in tissues from pressure imbalance, lymphatic block, or capillary leak.
  • RAAS — renin → angiotensin II → aldosterone; raises BP.
  • Aldosterone — reabsorbs Na & water, excretes K.
  • ADH — reabsorbs water only.
  • ANP / BNP — opposite of RAAS; excrete Na & water.
Electrolytes
  • Hyponatremia / Hypernatremia — brain affects: confusion, seizures.
  • Hypokalemia / Hyperkalemia — cardiac dysrhythmias; EKG changes.
  • Hypocalcemia — Trousseau's, Chvostek's, tetany.
  • Hypercalcemia — "bones, stones, groans, psychic moans."
  • Trousseau's sign — carpal spasm with BP cuff.
  • Chvostek's sign — facial twitch when cheek tapped.
Acid-Base & ABGs
  • Acidosis — pH < 7.35. Alkalosis — pH > 7.45.
  • CO₂ = acid. HCO₃⁻ = base.
  • Kussmaul respirations — deep rapid breathing in DKA.
  • Buffer systems — serum, respiratory, renal.
  • Anion gap — high in DKA, lactic acidosis, salicylates.
  • Compensation — the OTHER value shifts to bring pH back toward normal.
Pathogens & Infection
  • Gram positive — thick wall, purple. Gram negative — thin wall, pink, harder to treat.
  • Antibiotic resistance — misuse, incomplete courses, agricultural use.
  • TB latent vs active — walled off vs spreading; only active is contagious.
  • Stages of infection — Exposure → Incubation → Prodrome → Acute → Convalescent → Resolution.
  • Cystitis — bladder infection, no fever. Pyelonephritis — kidney, fever + flank pain.
  • Urosepsis — bloodstream infection from UTI.
Immune System
  • Innate — fast, non-specific, no memory. Adaptive — slow, specific, memory.
  • B cells — make antibodies. T cells — CD4 helpers, CD8 cytotoxic.
  • WBCs — neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils.
  • Immunoglobulins (GAMED) — G long-term, A access/mucous, M first responder, E allergy, D naive B cells.
  • Hypersensitivity I–IV — allergic, antiBody, immune complex, delayed.
  • Anaphylaxis — Type I; treat with IM epinephrine.
  • Immunity types — natural/artificial × active/passive.
Immune Disorders & Cancers
  • HIV — retrovirus targeting CD4 cells.
  • AIDS — CD4 < 200 or AIDS-defining opportunistic infection.
  • Opportunistic infections — PCP, oral thrush, toxoplasmosis, Kaposi sarcoma.
  • Autoimmune — immune attacks self (lupus, RA, Type 1 DM).
  • Immunodeficiency — immune too weak (HIV, chemo, malnutrition, splenectomy).
  • Leukemia — cancer of WBC precursors; lymphoblastic (B/T) or myelocytic.
  • Hodgkin lymphoma — Reed-Sternberg cells, localized, better prognosis.
  • Non-Hodgkin lymphoma — diffuse, no Reed-Sternberg, older, worse prognosis.

🎯 Final Study Tips for Exam 1

  • Memorize lab values first. If you don't know the normal ranges, you can't recognize abnormal patterns.
  • Practice ABGs until they're automatic. Do all 4 worked examples, then make up your own.
  • Make a single-page table with electrolytes vs. high/low s/sx side-by-side. Re-write it without looking.
  • Connect mechanisms to s/sx. Don't memorize lists — understand WHY hyperkalemia peaks T waves, WHY hypocalcemia causes tetany.
  • Practice with the hidden answers. Active recall beats passive re-reading every time.
  • Use the cheat sheet the night before AND morning of the exam.
  • Set up your testing environment the day before — lock down browser installed, room scanned, whiteboard ready, no devices.
  • Don't rush. 50 questions, plenty of time. Read each option carefully — SATA needs ALL correct answers.
