🧬 The Big Picture: Two Modules

TOPIC 1 😞 Mood Disorders: Depression & Bipolar MUST KNOW▼

Depression (Major Depressive Disorder, MDD)

Persistent feelings of sadness or apathy lasting 2+ weeks. Common symptoms: low energy, sleep problems, abnormal eating, despair, guilt, hopelessness, and suicidal ideation. Subtypes:

  • Postpartum depression — up to 80% of mothers get mild "baby blues" in the first 2 weeks; ~10–15% develop more severe PPD; linked to hormonal changes.
  • Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD) — occurs mostly in winter (less sunlight); associated with decreased melatonin and serotonin.
  • Dysthymia — mild depression lasting longer than 2 years.

Causes of Depression (multifactorial)

CategoryExamples
BiologicalNeurotransmitter imbalance (serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine), hormones, low sunlight/vitamin D.
GeneticFamily history of mood disorders.
PsychosocialTrauma, grief, chronic stress, isolation.
MedicalChronic illness, medication side effects, postpartum changes.
High-yield safety point: suicide risk is highest when treatment begins to work — energy returns before mood fully lifts, so a person now has the energy to act on suicidal thoughts.

Bipolar Disorder

A serious disorder with a higher-than-average suicide risk, defined by extreme mood swings between mania and depression:

  • Mania — grandiose ideas, decreased need for sleep/food, distractibility, impaired judgment (spending, risky sexual behavior), pressured speech, possibly delusions/paranoia/hallucinations.
  • Depression — the classic depressive symptoms above.
  • Cyclothymia — milder emotional highs and lows.
💡 Mnemonic: Depression neurotransmitters = "Sad people lack SND" (Serotonin, Norepinephrine, Dopamine). SAD = Sun-Absence Depression (winter, low melatonin/serotonin). Mania = "DIG FAST" (Distractibility, Insomnia/↓sleep, Grandiosity, Flight of ideas, Activity↑, Speech pressured, Thoughtlessness/risk).
⚠️ Common confusion: Suicide risk peaks as a depressed patient starts to improve (regains energy before mood lifts) — not at the lowest point. And bipolar requires episodes of mania (not just depression); without mania it's major depression.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. How long must low mood persist to meet criteria for major depressive disorder?
    Show answer
    At least 2 weeks of persistent sadness or apathy.
  2. Why does seasonal affective disorder occur mostly in winter?
    Show answer
    Reduced sunlight alters melatonin and serotonin, lowering mood during the dark winter months.
  3. SATA: Which are signs of a manic episode? (A) Decreased need for sleep (B) Pressured speech (C) Grandiose ideas (D) Excessive guilt and apathy (E) Impulsive spending
    Show answer
    A, B, C, E. Excessive guilt and apathy are depressive, not manic.
  4. When is a depressed client at highest risk for suicide?
    Show answer
    When treatment starts to work and energy returns before mood fully improves.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A client with severe depression starts an antidepressant. Two weeks later they seem more energetic and are being closely monitored for safety. Why the extra caution now?
Show answer
Antidepressants often restore energy and motivation before they lift mood. A still-hopeless patient who now has energy may be at the highest risk for acting on suicidal thoughts, so close monitoring is critical in the early treatment window.
☑ Self-check: Can you define MDD (2 weeks), explain SAD, list manic signs (DIG FAST), and state when suicide risk peaks? If yes, move on.
TOPIC 2 🧠 Anxiety, Schizophrenia & ADHD MUST KNOW▼

Anxiety Disorders

Excessive fear or worry that interferes with daily function — with both psychological AND physiological effects (rapid heart rate, hypertension, restlessness). Types:

TypeKey feature
GADGeneralized, persistent worry and fear.
Social anxietyFear of public/social interaction.
OCDUnwanted, recurrent behaviors performed to reduce anxiety.
Panic disorderSudden intense emotional unrest triggered by stress, fear, or exertion.
PTSDFollows a traumatic event; varied psychological/physical effects.

Schizophrenia

A chronic, severe disorder that impairs perception of reality, thought patterns, and functioning. Linked to dopamine dysregulation. Symptoms split into two groups:

Positive (added) — excess dopamineNegative (lost) — low dopamine
Hallucinations, delusions, disorganized speech, paranoia (psychosis)Alogia (no speech), avolition (no motivation), anhedonia (no pleasure), social withdrawal

Positive = adds something abnormal (hallucinations); negative = takes normal function away (no motivation).

ADHD

A neurodevelopmental disorder usually beginning in childhood (can persist into adulthood), from dysregulation of dopamine and norepinephrine (these are low). Core symptoms: inattention, hyperactivity, impulsiveness. Risk factors: genetics, traumatic brain injury, in-utero toxins (lead, alcohol, cigarette smoke). Associated: low self-esteem, social/academic difficulty.

💡 Mnemonic: Schizophrenia: "Positive = Presence (hallucinations/delusions, too much dopamine); Negative = Nothing (the 3 A's: Alogia, Avolition, Anhedonia, too little dopamine)." ADHD = "low Dopamine & Norepinephrine" (which is why stimulants that raise them help).
⚠️ Common confusion: In schizophrenia, positive symptoms come from EXCESS dopamine (treated with dopamine blockers) while negative symptoms come from LOW dopamine. "Positive" doesn't mean good — it means symptoms added to normal experience.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. Which anxiety disorder involves recurrent behaviors performed to relieve anxiety?
    Show answer
    Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD).
  2. SATA: Which are POSITIVE symptoms of schizophrenia? (A) Avolition (B) Hallucinations (C) Paranoia (D) Social withdrawal (E) Delusions
    Show answer
    B, C, E. Avolition and social withdrawal are negative symptoms.
  3. Which neurotransmitters are dysregulated (low) in ADHD?
    Show answer
    Dopamine and norepinephrine.
  4. What are the three core symptoms of ADHD?
    Show answer
    Inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsiveness.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A child with ADHD is prescribed a stimulant medication, which seems counterintuitive ("why give a stimulant to a hyperactive child?"). Using the neurotransmitter basis, explain why it works.
Show answer
ADHD involves low dopamine and norepinephrine in brain regions controlling attention and impulse. Stimulants raise these neurotransmitters, improving focus and self-control — so they calm symptoms rather than worsen hyperactivity.
☑ Self-check: Can you match the anxiety subtypes, split schizophrenia into positive (excess dopamine) vs negative (low dopamine), and give ADHD's neurotransmitters + symptoms? If yes, move on.
TOPIC 3 🫀 Increased ICP, Cushing's Triad & Headaches MUST KNOW▼

Increased Intracranial Pressure (ICP)

ICP is pressure inside the rigid skull. The skull holds 3 things (the Monro-Kellie idea): brain tissue, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Since the skull can't expand, if any one increases, pressure rises and "suffocates" the brain. Causes:

Component ↑Examples
BloodVasodilation (high CO&sub2;), venous obstruction, very high BP, hemorrhage.
Brain tissueTumor, edema (from injury/bleed).
CSFHydrocephalus (blocked drainage or overproduction).

Early vs. Late Signs

EarlyLate (ominous)
Headache, nausea, vomiting, drowsiness, confusion, double visionCushing's Triad, fixed/dilated pupils (CN III compression), decreased LOC, abnormal posturing, coma

Cushing's Triad (late, life-threatening)

Cushing's Triad = ↑ BP (widening pulse pressure) · ↓ HR (bradycardia) · ↓ RR (irregular)
A sign the brainstem is being compressed — a neurological emergency.

Posturing reflects severe damage: Decorticate = arms flexed toward the core ("de-COR-ticate, arms to the core"); Decerebrate = arms extended (worse).

Headache Types

TypeHallmark
Tension (most common)Bilateral "tight band" around the head; from tight muscles, poor posture, stress.
SinusFrontal/below the eyes; facial pain, often with a cold/congestion.
ClusterUnilateral, stabbing around one eye; eye tearing, nasal congestion (a type of migraine).
MigraineSevere, unilateral, pulsating/throbbing; nausea/vomiting, photophobia & phonophobia; worse with movement.
💡 Mnemonic: Skull's 3 contents: "Blood, Brain, CSF." Cushing's Triad = "high BP, low & slow (HR & RR)" — the opposite of shock. Decorticate = to the CORE (flexed); decerebrate = extended (worse). Cluster = "Clusters around one eye"; tension = "Tight band, both sides."
⚠️ Common confusion: Cushing's Triad (a sign of high ICP) is high BP + LOW HR + LOW/irregular RR — do NOT confuse it with shock (low BP + high HR). Cushing's Triad is a late finding; by the time it appears, herniation may be imminent.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. What are the 3 components inside the skull?
    Show answer
    Brain tissue, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).
  2. Name the 3 components of Cushing's Triad.
    Show answer
    Increased BP (widening pulse pressure), decreased heart rate (bradycardia), decreased/irregular respiratory rate.
  3. SATA: Which are LATE signs of increased ICP? (A) Hypertension (B) Bradycardia (C) Mild headache (D) Decreased respiratory rate (E) Fixed/dilated pupils
    Show answer
    A, B, D, E. Mild headache is an early sign.
  4. A patient has stabbing pain around one eye with tearing and nasal congestion. Headache type?
    Show answer
    Cluster headache.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A head-injury patient who was talking now has a BP of 190/70, HR of 48, and slow irregular breathing. Why is this an emergency, and what's happening?
Show answer
These vitals are Cushing's Triad (high BP/widening pulse pressure, bradycardia, slow irregular breathing) — a late sign of dangerously high ICP with brainstem compression. The brain is being squeezed and herniation may be imminent; this requires immediate intervention to lower ICP.
☑ Self-check: Can you list the skull's 3 contents, recite Cushing's Triad, distinguish early vs late ICP signs, and match the 4 headache types? If yes, move on.
TOPIC 4 🪹 Stroke (CVA), TIA & Bell's Palsy MUST KNOW▼

A stroke (cerebrovascular accident, CVA) is the death of brain cells from interrupted blood flow. There are two fundamentally different types — and telling them apart matters because the treatments are opposite.

Ischemic vs. Hemorrhagic

Ischemic (80–85%)Hemorrhagic (15–20%)
CauseBlockage of a vessel — thrombus (from atherosclerosis) or embolus (from A-fib)Rupture of a weakened vessel
Risk factorsAtherosclerosis, atrial fibrillation, clotsHypertension (#1), aneurysm, trauma
Classic signsFacial drooping, one-sided weakness, speech changes, confusionSudden severe ("thunderclap") headache, sudden vomiting or LOC
TreatmentFibrinolytics (clot busters) — only after CT rules out a bleed!Stop the bleeding / control BP (NO clot busters)
Why CT first: a clot-busting drug given to a hemorrhagic stroke would be catastrophic — so a head CT must rule out a bleed before fibrinolytics.

TIA (Transient Ischemic Attack)

A temporary ischemic event where symptoms fully resolve with no permanent damage. It's a "warning sign" of a high likelihood of a bigger stroke. (Modern definition is tissue-based, not strictly time-based.)

Bell's Palsy

Unilateral paralysis of the facial nerve (cranial nerve VII). Cause usually unknown (herpes infection or Lyme suspected). Facial weakness ONLY — no symptoms in the arms or legs. This is the key way to tell it from a stroke:

Bell's palsy vs stroke: Bell's palsy affects the whole side of the face (including the forehead) with NO limb involvement. A stroke usually spares the forehead and often comes with arm/leg weakness and speech changes.
💡 Mnemonic: Stroke screen = "BE FAST" (Balance, Eyes, Face droop, Arm weakness, Speech, Time). Ischemic = "I-clog" (clot); Hemorrhagic = "Hemorrhage = bleed," thunderclap headache, #1 cause HTN. TIA = "Temporary, a warning." Bell's palsy = "face only, no limbs."
⚠️ Common confusion: Don't give fibrinolytics until a CT rules out a hemorrhagic stroke. And Bell's palsy involves the forehead and ONLY the face; if there's limb weakness or forehead sparing, think stroke instead.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. What causes an ischemic vs a hemorrhagic stroke?
    Show answer
    Ischemic = a blockage (thrombus/embolus); hemorrhagic = rupture of a weakened vessel (often from hypertension).
  2. Why must a CT be done before giving clot-busting drugs?
    Show answer
    To rule out a hemorrhagic stroke — fibrinolytics would worsen a bleed catastrophically.
  3. SATA: Which are true of a TIA? (A) Symptoms resolve (B) No permanent damage (C) It's a warning of higher stroke risk (D) It is hemorrhagic (E) Requires immediate surgery
    Show answer
    A, B, C. TIA is ischemic (not hemorrhagic) and doesn't require surgery.
  4. How can you distinguish Bell's palsy from a stroke?
    Show answer
    Bell's palsy causes facial weakness only (including the forehead) with no limb involvement; stroke usually spares the forehead and includes arm/leg weakness and speech changes.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient arrives with sudden one-sided weakness and slurred speech that started an hour ago. The team rushes a CT before any medication. Explain the reasoning and what treatment follows if the CT is clear.
Show answer
The symptoms suggest a stroke. A CT rules out a hemorrhagic stroke first, because giving fibrinolytics (clot busters) to a bleed would be deadly. If the CT shows no bleed (consistent with an ischemic stroke) and the patient is within the time window, fibrinolytics can be given to dissolve the clot — "time is brain."
☑ Self-check: Can you contrast ischemic vs hemorrhagic (cause, signs, treatment), define TIA, and distinguish Bell's palsy from stroke? If yes, move on.
TOPIC 5 🧤 TBI, Brain Bleeds, Hydrocephalus & Meningitis MUST KNOW▼

Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)

  • Concussion (mild TBI) — from impact/whiplash; nausea, vomiting, confusion, blurred vision, impaired coordination, headache, personality changes.
  • Contusion — a brain "bruise"; coup-contrecoup (injury at the impact site AND the opposite side).
  • Diffuse axonal injury — violent rotation shears axons; often causes unconsciousness or a persistent vegetative state.
  • Penetrating injury — an object enters the skull.

Brain Bleeds (Hematomas/Hemorrhage) — named by location

TypeWhere & speed
Epidural hematomaAbove the dura (between skull & dura); arterial — bleeds fast and severely.
Subdural hematomaBelow the dura (between dura & arachnoid); venous — usually slower.
Intracerebral hemorrhageWithin the brain tissue (a type of hemorrhagic stroke); from HTN/aneurysm/trauma.
Subarachnoid hemorrhageIn the subarachnoid space; often a ruptured berry aneurysm.
Decode the names: "epi-" = above, "sub-" = below; "dural" refers to the dura mater. Epidural = Arterial = fast ("E and A early"); Subdural = venous = slow.

Hydrocephalus ("Water on the Brain")

Accumulation of CSF in the ventricles (from blocked drainage — most common — or overproduction). Signs differ by age:

Infant (soft skull)Adult (fixed skull)
Increased head circumference, LOC changes, irritabilityHeadache, vomiting, ataxia, urinary incontinence, lethargy, coma

An infant's skull bones aren't fused, so the head enlarges; an adult's fused skull instead develops rising ICP signs.

Meningitis vs. Encephalitis

MeningitisEncephalitis
What's inflamedThe meninges (membranes)The brain tissue itself
CauseBacterial (more severe) or viral (often self-limiting)Usually viral (herpes most common)
HallmarkClassic triad: nuchal rigidity (stiff neck), severe headache, fever + photophobiaNonspecific: fever, headache, photophobia, confusion
💡 Mnemonic: "Epidural = Artery = fast & deadly; Subdural = vein = slow." Meningitis triad = "Stiff neck, Headache, Fever." Hydrocephalus: infant head grows; adult head can't, so pressure climbs. Encephalitis = "encephalo = brain tissue inflamed (confusion)."
⚠️ Common confusion: Meningitis = membranes; encephalitis = brain tissue (confusion is more prominent in encephalitis). And epidural bleeds are arterial (rapid deterioration), while subdural bleeds are venous (slower, common in elderly/alcoholics after falls).

❓ Practice Questions

  1. Which brain bleed is arterial and fast, and where is it?
    Show answer
    Epidural hematoma — arterial bleed above the dura (between skull and dura mater).
  2. Why does hydrocephalus increase head circumference in infants but not adults?
    Show answer
    Infant skull bones aren't fused, so the head expands; an adult's fused skull can't, so ICP rises (headache, vomiting, ataxia) instead.
  3. What is the classic triad of meningitis?
    Show answer
    Nuchal rigidity (stiff neck), severe headache, and fever.
  4. A boxer is hit and the brain is injured both at the blow site and the opposite side. What is this called?
    Show answer
    Coup-contrecoup (a contusion pattern).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
After a head injury, a patient is briefly knocked out, wakes up and feels fine ("lucid interval"), then rapidly deteriorates into unconsciousness. Which bleed does this classically suggest, and why the rapid decline?
Show answer
An epidural hematoma. It's an arterial bleed, so after a brief "lucid interval" the rapidly expanding blood raises ICP quickly and compresses the brain — causing fast deterioration. It's a neurosurgical emergency.
☑ Self-check: Can you classify TBIs, locate the 4 brain bleeds (epidural=arterial/fast), explain hydrocephalus by age, and contrast meningitis vs encephalitis? If yes, move on.
TOPIC 6 🦴 Spinal Cord Injury, Shock & Seizures MUST KNOW▼

Spinal Cord Injury (SCI)

Damage that interrupts motor, sensory, or autonomic pathways. Most-injured areas: cervical and lumbar (most mobile). The level determines the deficit:

  • Quadriplegia — injury at C8 and above (all four limbs). "C5 keeps the diaphragm alive" — injury above C5 can stop breathing.
  • Paraplegia — injury at T1 and below (legs). Injuries at T6 and above can cause neurogenic shock (bradycardia).

Injury types (by mechanism): flexion (head-on MVA), hyperextension (hitting chin in a fall), compression (diving into shallow water, object on head), subluxation (falling off a horse).

Spinal Shock vs. Neurogenic Shock

Spinal ShockNeurogenic Shock
What it isTemporary loss of reflexes & flaccidity below injuryLoss of sympathetic outflow → hemodynamic instability (injury usually above T6)
SignsAbsent deep tendon reflexes, flaccid paralysis, loss of sensation below injuryHypotension + bradycardia (unopposed parasympathetic tone)
CourseWon't know extent of injury for 4–6 weeksOften resolves in 24–48 hrs once stabilized
The tell: neurogenic shock's bradycardia (slow HR) is unusual — most other shocks cause a fast HR. It's from losing sympathetic ("fight or flight") tone, leaving the vagus nerve unopposed.

Autonomic Dysreflexia (a later SCI complication)

A dangerous sympathetic overdrive in patients with SCI at T6 or higher, triggered by an irritant below the injury (full bladder, constipation, tight clothing). The body can't coordinate its response across the injury:

  • Above the injury: vasodilation → forehead sweating, facial flushing, pounding headache.
  • Below the injury: vasoconstriction → severe hypertension.
  • The brain senses the high BP and tries to compensate with bradycardia. Emergency — find and remove the trigger!

Seizures & Spina Bifida

  • Seizure — sudden, disorganized electrical discharge in the brain. Status epilepticus = a seizure lasting >5 minutes OR 2+ seizures within 5 minutes (an emergency).
  • Spina bifida — improper closing of the spinal column in development, caused by maternal folate deficiency; prevented by taking folate. Types: occulta (hidden, minimal), meningocele, myelomeningocele (most severe, often lower-limb paralysis).
💡 Mnemonic: "C5 keeps the diaphragm alive." Neurogenic/autonomic dysreflexia both cause bradycardia + a BP problem (think "the slow-heart shock"). Autonomic dysreflexia = "find the irritant below (bladder, bowel, tight clothes)." Folate prevents spina bifida. Status epilepticus = "5 minutes is too long."
⚠️ Common confusion: Both neurogenic shock and autonomic dysreflexia cause bradycardia, but neurogenic shock has low BP while autonomic dysreflexia has severe HIGH BP. Spinal shock (loss of reflexes/flaccidity) is different from neurogenic shock (cardiovascular instability).

❓ Practice Questions

  1. What does "C5 keeps the diaphragm alive" mean?
    Show answer
    The diaphragm is innervated around C3–C5 (phrenic nerve); injuries above C5 can paralyze the diaphragm and stop breathing.
  2. Distinguish spinal shock from neurogenic shock.
    Show answer
    Spinal shock = temporary loss of reflexes and flaccidity below the injury; neurogenic shock = loss of sympathetic tone causing hypotension and bradycardia.
  3. SATA: Which are signs of autonomic dysreflexia? (A) Forehead sweating (B) Severe hypertension (C) Bradycardia (D) Hypotension (E) Pounding headache
    Show answer
    A, B, C, E. Autonomic dysreflexia causes severe HYPERtension, not hypotension.
  4. Define status epilepticus.
    Show answer
    A seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, or two or more seizures within 5 minutes without recovery.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with a C5 spinal cord injury suddenly develops a pounding headache, flushing and sweating on the face, and a BP of 210/110. What's happening, and what's the first thing to look for?
Show answer
This is autonomic dysreflexia (SCI at T6 or above). An irritant below the injury (most commonly a full bladder, then constipation or tight clothing) triggers sympathetic overdrive and dangerous hypertension. Immediately find and remove the trigger (check/empty the bladder first) and sit the patient up to lower BP.
☑ Self-check: Can you tie SCI level to quadri-/paraplegia, contrast spinal vs neurogenic shock, explain autonomic dysreflexia (high BP + brady), and define status epilepticus? If yes, you've finished Module 5!
TOPIC 7 🩹 Trauma Assessment & Spinal Curvatures MUST KNOW▼

Primary Survey (ABCDE) — for trauma

Used to immediately identify life-threatening conditions in trauma, especially in unresponsive clients. Work through it in order:

LetterCheck
A — AirwayIs the airway occluded?
B — BreathingRespiratory distress? Normal breath sounds?
C — CirculationHeart rate, pallor, capillary refill.
D — DisabilityNeurologic status — can they respond?
E — Exposure/EnvironmentExpose to find injuries; environmental (e.g., hypothermia).

Priority Assessment (the 5 P's) — for fractures/dislocations

Used to check the health of tissue around a fracture or dislocation — has the bone damaged nearby nerves, arteries, or veins?

PMeaning
PainCan they detect pain?
PulseIs blood circulating?
PallorPale = poor perfusion.
ParesthesiaCan they feel (sensation)?
ParalysisCan they move?

Spinal Curvatures

CurveDescription
KyphosisExaggerated forward rounding of the upper back ("hunchback"). Common with osteoporosis.
LordosisExaggerated inward curve of the lower back ("swayback").
ScoliosisSideways (lateral) curvature of the spine.
💡 Mnemonic: Trauma = "ABCDE" (Airway first, always). Fracture = "5 P's" (Pain, Pulse, Pallor, Paresthesia, Paralysis). Curves: "Kyphosis = K looks hunched forward; Lordosis = Looking up/swayback; Scoliosis = S-shaped sideways."
⚠️ Common confusion: The primary survey (ABCDE) is for whole-body trauma/life threats; the priority assessment (5 P's) is for limb/tissue health around a fracture. Kyphosis = forward hunch, lordosis = inward sway, scoliosis = sideways — don't mix them up.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. What do the letters in the trauma primary survey stand for?
    Show answer
    Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure/Environment.
  2. List the 5 P's of the priority (neurovascular) assessment.
    Show answer
    Pain, Pulse, Pallor, Paresthesia, Paralysis.
  3. Which spinal curvature is a sideways curve?
    Show answer
    Scoliosis.

✅ Multiple Choice Practice

  1. In the primary survey, what is assessed FIRST?
    A) Circulation · B) Disability · C) Airway · D) Exposure
    Show answer
    C. Airway (the "A" in ABCDE).
  2. Exaggerated forward rounding of the upper back is:
    A) Lordosis · B) Scoliosis · C) Kyphosis · D) Stenosis
    Show answer
    C. Kyphosis.
  3. The 5 P's assessment is used for:
    A) Mental status · B) Fractures/dislocations · C) Stroke · D) Burns
    Show answer
    B. Assessing tissue around a fracture or dislocation.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with a casted forearm fracture reports increasing numbness and the fingers look pale with a weak pulse. Using the 5 P's, why is this urgent?
Show answer
Pallor, paresthesia (numbness), and a weak pulse are abnormal findings in the 5 P's neurovascular check — they signal that blood flow and nerve function to the limb are compromised (possible compartment syndrome). This is an emergency requiring immediate evaluation to prevent permanent tissue/nerve damage.

TOPIC 8 🦴 Bone Disorders MUST KNOW▼

Four bone disorders that are easy to mix up. The key is the underlying problem for each.

DisorderCore problem & key facts
OsteoporosisDemineralization — osteoclast (breakdown) activity > osteoblast (building). Bones become porous/brittle. Risk factors: menopause/low estrogen, age, Caucasian/Asian race, smoking, alcohol, steroids, low calcium, sedentary lifestyle, hyperthyroidism. Signs: bone pain (back), kyphosis, height loss, fracture risk.
Osteomalacia"Soft bone" — impaired mineralization from vitamin D deficiency (called rickets in children). Linked to low calcium/phosphate, high PTH, renal disease. Signs: bone pain, deformities (bowed legs, pigeon breast).
Osteoarthritis (OA)Non-inflammatory "wear and tear" — cartilage erodes, bone rubs on bone. Irreversible, progresses slowly. Pain WORSENS as the day goes on / with use; usually unilateral. Risk: age, female, obesity, prior injury, repetitive joint use.
OsteomyelitisBacterial infection IN the bone — high risk with open fractures (skin/environment contact). Signs: constant bone pain not relieved by rest, fever, chills, night sweats, warmth/tenderness. Not seen on early x-ray; untreated → sepsis.
💡 Mnemonic: "OsteoporOsis = pOrOus bones (too few minerals)." "OsteoMALACIA = Malformed/soft (vitamin D); in kids = Rickets." "OsteoMYELitis = microbes (infection), think MY bone is infected." OA = "Old joints, worse with use" (wear & tear; remember RA was worse in the morning, OA worse by evening).
⚠️ Common confusion: Osteoporosis = too little bone quantity (demineralization); osteomalacia = poor bone quality/softening (vitamin D). Osteoarthritis pain worsens through the day (vs rheumatoid arthritis, which improves through the day). Osteomyelitis is an infection — constant pain + fever.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. What is the underlying cause of osteomalacia?
    Show answer
    Vitamin D deficiency → impaired bone mineralization ("soft bone"); it's called rickets in children.
  2. How does osteoarthritis pain change over the day, and how does that differ from RA?
    Show answer
    OA pain worsens as the day/use goes on; rheumatoid arthritis stiffness is worst in the morning and improves with activity.
  3. SATA: Which are risk factors for osteoporosis? (A) Menopause/low estrogen (B) Smoking (C) Prolonged steroid use (D) Regular weight-bearing exercise (E) Low calcium intake
    Show answer
    A, B, C, E. Weight-bearing exercise protects against osteoporosis.
  4. Which bone disorder is a bacterial infection, and what's a major risk factor?
    Show answer
    Osteomyelitis; open fractures (which expose bone to skin/environmental bacteria).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A postmenopausal woman has a vertebral compression fracture from a minor fall, with kyphosis and height loss. Which bone disorder is most likely, and why does menopause increase the risk?
Show answer
Osteoporosis. Estrogen helps maintain bone density; after menopause, estrogen drops, so osteoclast (breakdown) activity outpaces osteoblast (building) activity. Bones become porous and brittle, leading to fragility fractures, kyphosis, and height loss from even minor trauma.
☑ Self-check: Can you give the core problem for osteoporosis, osteomalacia, OA, and osteomyelitis, and the OA-vs-RA timing difference? If yes, move on.
TOPIC 9 🪥 Fracture Complications & Soft Tissue Injuries MUST KNOW▼

Three Dangerous Fracture Complications

ComplicationPatho & signs
Compartment syndromePressure inside a muscle compartment gets so high (from edema) it compresses nerves/vessels → ischemia. 6 P's: severe sudden Pain, Paresthesia, Pallor, Paralysis, Pulselessness, Poikilothermia (cool). Emergency!
Fat embolismFat globules from yellow bone marrow enter the blood and occlude vessels. Triad: Confusion (brain), Petechiae (chest/upper torso rash), Shortness of breath (lung). Common with long-bone fractures.
RhabdomyolysisRapid muscle breakdown (overuse, trauma, crush, statins) releases myoglobin + potassium into the blood. Triad: Myalgia (muscle pain), brown urine (myoglobinuria), weakness. Labs: high creatine kinase, hyperkalemia. Can cause kidney injury.
Osteomyelitis (Topic 8) is also a fracture complication — the infection risk from open fractures.

Soft Tissue Injuries

InjuryWhat's torn/stretched
SprainA ligament (bone-to-bone) — from a quick twist/pull; signs: bruising, swelling, instability, painful movement. (Common at joints, e.g., ankle.)
StrainA muscle or tendon — common in large muscle groups (low back, calf, hamstring).
Rotator cuff injuryShoulder; pain with abduction, popping/clicking, muscle weakness.
ACL tearKnee ligament; classic "plant and twist" non-contact injury; increases risk of later osteoarthritis.
💡 Mnemonic: Compartment syndrome = "6 P's" (the 5 P's + Poikilothermia). Fat embolism triad = "Confused, Spotty, Breathless" (brain, petechiae, lungs). Rhabdo = "Muscle pain + brown pee." Sprain vs strain: "Sprain = ligament (s for skeletal joint); Strain = muscle/tendon (t for tendon)."
⚠️ Common confusion: Sprain = ligament; strain = muscle/tendon. Compartment syndrome's pain is severe and out of proportion, with the 6 P's; don't dismiss it as normal fracture pain. Brown urine after a crush/overuse injury = rhabdomyolysis (myoglobin), a clue for hyperkalemia and kidney risk.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. What is the classic triad of a fat embolism?
    Show answer
    Confusion, petechiae (upper torso), and shortness of breath.
  2. What's the difference between a sprain and a strain?
    Show answer
    A sprain is a stretched/torn ligament; a strain is a stretched/torn muscle or tendon.
  3. SATA: Which are signs of compartment syndrome? (A) Severe sudden pain (B) Paresthesia (C) Pallor (D) Strong bounding pulse (E) Pulselessness
    Show answer
    A, B, C, E. Compartment syndrome causes pulselessness, not a strong pulse.
  4. What turns the urine brown in rhabdomyolysis, and what dangerous electrolyte is released?
    Show answer
    Myoglobin (from broken-down muscle) darkens the urine; potassium is released, risking hyperkalemia.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A few days after a femur fracture, a patient suddenly becomes confused and short of breath, with a pinpoint rash across the chest. What complication is this, and why does a long-bone fracture cause it?
Show answer
A fat embolism. Long bones (like the femur) contain yellow (fatty) marrow; a fracture can release fat globules into the bloodstream, which travel to the lungs (dyspnea), brain (confusion), and skin (petechiae) — the classic triad.
☑ Self-check: Can you give the 6 P's, the fat embolism triad, the rhabdo triad, and the sprain-vs-strain distinction? If yes, move on.
TOPIC 10 🦠 Skin Infections (Bacterial, Viral & Fungal) MUST KNOW▼

Bacterial Skin Infections

Most are caused by Staphylococcus or Streptococcus. The key distinction is how deep they go:

InfectionDepth & hallmark
ImpetigoSuperficial; classic honey-colored crusts; very contagious (common in children).
CellulitisDeep infection of the dermis & subcutaneous tissue (hypodermis); red, warm, swollen, tender; drainage is contagious. Can spread to blood (sepsis).
Depth matters: impetigo = surface (epidermis); cellulitis = deep (dermis + subcutaneous). That's why cellulitis is more dangerous.

Viral Skin Infections

  • Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) — painful vesicular (blister) rash, often with tingling/numbness before a flare. HSV-1 = oral ("cold sores"); HSV-2 = genital/sexual contact. Can spread even when asymptomatic; can pass to a baby during delivery (often a C-section is done).
  • Measles — a respiratory virus that affects the skin: a maculopapular rash that starts on the head and descends. Plus high fever (104°F), cough, conjunctivitis, coryza (runny nose), and Koplik spots (white spots inside the cheeks — pathognomonic).

Fungal Skin Infections

Superficial fungi (e.g., tinea "ringworm," candida) love warm, moist areas (skin folds, feet, groin). Deep fungal infections occur mainly in immunocompromised patients.

💡 Mnemonic: "Impetigo = honey crust, surface; Cellulitis = deep & dangerous." Measles = the "3 C's + Koplik" (Cough, Coryza, Conjunctivitis + Koplik spots), rash head-to-toe. HSV-1 = mouth (1 mouth); HSV-2 = below the belt. Fungus loves "warm & moist."
⚠️ Common confusion: Impetigo is superficial (honey crusts); cellulitis is deep (dermis/subcutaneous). Koplik spots (inside the mouth) are the giveaway for measles. And both impetigo and cellulitis are usually Staph/Strep — bacterial, not viral.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. Which bacterial skin infection is deep (dermis/subcutaneous) and which is superficial with honey crusts?
    Show answer
    Cellulitis is deep; impetigo is superficial with honey-colored crusts.
  2. What are Koplik spots a sign of?
    Show answer
    Measles (white spots inside the mouth/cheeks).
  3. Differentiate HSV-1 from HSV-2.
    Show answer
    HSV-1 is typically oral (cold sores); HSV-2 is typically genital (sexual/skin-to-skin contact).
  4. Where do fungal skin infections like to grow?
    Show answer
    Warm, moist areas (skin folds, feet, groin).
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient has a red, hot, swollen, tender lower leg with poorly defined borders, spreading over a day, plus fever. Which infection is this, and why is it more concerning than impetigo?
Show answer
Cellulitis. Unlike superficial impetigo, cellulitis is a deep infection of the dermis and subcutaneous tissue. Because it's deep and can spread into the bloodstream, it risks sepsis and needs prompt antibiotics — the fever signals possible systemic spread.
☑ Self-check: Can you separate impetigo (superficial) vs cellulitis (deep), recall measles' signs (3 C's + Koplik), distinguish HSV-1/2, and say where fungi grow? If yes, move on.
TOPIC 11 🧶 Chronic Skin Conditions & Skin Cancers MUST KNOW▼

Chronic / Inflammatory Skin Conditions

ConditionPatho & hallmark
Eczema (atopic dermatitis)Chronic inflammatory; dry, itchy, red skin. Common in childhood; classically on flexor surfaces (behind the knees/popliteal, inner elbow/antecubital). Linked to asthma & allergies.
Urticaria (hives)Itchy "wheal and flare" rash; IgE-mediated histamine release — seen with allergic reactions.
Acne vulgarisInflammatory disorder of the pilosebaceous unit: increased sebum, follicle blockage by keratin, and bacteria (Propionibacterium / Cutibacterium).
PsoriasisChronic autoimmune; thick, silvery, scaly plaques (rapid skin-cell turnover).

Skin Cancers

CancerBehavior
Basal cell carcinomaMost common; rarely metastasizes (least dangerous).
Squamous cell carcinomaCan metastasize to nodes/organs.
MelanomaMost dangerous; high risk of metastasis. Watch the ABCDEs.

Melanoma: the ABCDEs

LetterWarning sign
A — AsymmetryOne half doesn't match the other.
B — BorderIrregular, ragged, or blurred edges.
C — ColorVaried colors within the lesion.
D — DiameterLarger than ~6 mm (pencil eraser).
E — EvolvingChanging in size, shape, or color over time.
💡 Mnemonic: Melanoma = "ABCDE" (Asymmetry, Border, Color, Diameter, Evolving). Cancer danger order: "Basal = best (rarely spreads), Melanoma = most malignant." Eczema = flexor surfaces + itch + asthma/allergy; Urticaria = IgE/histamine hives (allergy). Acne = "sebum + plug + bacteria."
⚠️ Common confusion: Basal cell = most common but least likely to spread; melanoma = less common but most deadly. Urticaria is IgE/histamine-mediated (think allergy), while eczema is a chronic inflammatory condition on flexor surfaces.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. What do the ABCDEs of melanoma stand for?
    Show answer
    Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Color variation, Diameter (>6 mm), Evolving.
  2. Which skin cancer is most dangerous, and which is most common but rarely spreads?
    Show answer
    Melanoma is most dangerous; basal cell carcinoma is most common but rarely metastasizes.
  3. What mediates urticaria, and when is it commonly seen?
    Show answer
    It's IgE-mediated histamine release ("wheal and flare"), commonly with allergic reactions.
  4. Where does eczema classically appear, and what conditions is it linked to?
    Show answer
    On flexor surfaces (behind the knees, inner elbows); linked to asthma and allergies.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient points out a mole that's now asymmetric, has ragged borders, several colors, is 8 mm wide, and has changed over a few months. What should the nurse suspect, and why the urgency?
Show answer
The mole hits every ABCDE warning sign — highly suspicious for melanoma. Melanoma has a high risk of metastasis, so early recognition and biopsy/removal are critical; delay allows it to spread and become far harder to treat.

TOPIC 12 🔥 Ulcers & Burns MUST KNOW▼

Pressure Ulcers vs. Venous Stasis Ulcers

Pressure Ulcer (decubitus)Venous Stasis Ulcer
CauseIschemia from unrelieved pressure over a bony area → skin breakdown.Dysfunctional vein valves → fluid & waste accumulate in the legs → edema and tissue damage.
Risk factorsImmobility, pressure, shearing, friction, moisture; poor nutrition, aging, steroids.Chronic venous insufficiency, leg swelling, varicose veins.
Typical locationOver bony prominences (sacrum, heels, hips).Lower legs (around the ankles).

Pressure ulcer factors split into extrinsic (pressure, shear, friction, moisture) and intrinsic (immobility, poor nutrition, chronic illness, aging).

Burns — Depth Classification

DegreeDepth & appearance
Superficial (1st degree)Epidermis only; red, painful, no blisters (like a sunburn).
Partial thickness (2nd degree)Epidermis + part of dermis; red, blistered, very painful.
Full thickness (3rd degree)Through the entire dermis (into fat); white/charred/leathery; often painless in the center (nerves destroyed).

Burn Complications

  • Infection / sepsis — the skin barrier is gone.
  • Hypovolemic shock — massive fluid loss/leak → edema, hemodynamic instability, and pre-renal acute kidney injury.
  • Hypothermia — loss of temperature regulation.
  • Respiratory dysfunction — from inhalation injury.
  • Increased metabolic demand (hypermetabolic state).
💡 Mnemonic: "Pressure ulcer = Pressure + bony spot; Venous ulcer = Valves fail in the legs." Burn depth: "1st = red, 2nd = blisters, 3rd = no feeling (nerves gone)." Burn's biggest early killer = hypovolemic shock (fluid loss); biggest later killer = infection/sepsis.
⚠️ Common confusion: A full-thickness (3rd degree) burn is often painLESS in the center because the nerve endings are destroyed — so lack of pain does NOT mean a minor burn. Pressure ulcers are from pressure/ischemia over bone; venous stasis ulcers are from failing vein valves in the legs.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. What causes a pressure ulcer vs a venous stasis ulcer?
    Show answer
    Pressure ulcer = ischemia from unrelieved pressure over a bony area; venous stasis ulcer = dysfunctional vein valves causing fluid/waste buildup and tissue damage in the legs.
  2. Why is a full-thickness (3rd degree) burn often painless centrally?
    Show answer
    The nerve endings in the dermis are destroyed, so the center doesn't transmit pain.
  3. SATA: Which are complications of major burns? (A) Infection/sepsis (B) Hypovolemic shock (C) Hypothermia (D) Weight gain (E) Acute kidney injury
    Show answer
    A, B, C, E. Burns cause a hypermetabolic state and weight loss, not gain.
  4. Which burn depth has blisters and is very painful?
    Show answer
    Partial-thickness (2nd degree) — epidermis + part of dermis.
🩺 Clinical Scenario
A patient with extensive burns develops low blood pressure, low urine output, and rising kidney values within hours. Connect the burn to these findings.
Show answer
Major burns cause huge fluid shifts and plasma loss through damaged skin → hypovolemic shock (low BP). Reduced blood flow to the kidneys causes pre-renal acute kidney injury (low urine output, rising creatinine). This is why aggressive fluid resuscitation is a top early priority in burn care.
☑ Self-check: Can you contrast pressure vs venous stasis ulcers, classify the 3 burn depths, and list the major burn complications? If yes — all 12 topics done!

🎯 Exam Cheat Sheet

🧠 MODULE 5 — Mental Health & Neuro
  • Depression: 2+ weeks; low serotonin/NE/dopamine. Suicide risk highest as treatment starts to work. SAD = winter/low melatonin.
  • Bipolar: mania (DIG FAST) + depression; high suicide risk. Schizophrenia: Positive = excess dopamine (hallucinations); Negative = low dopamine (3 A's: alogia, avolition, anhedonia).
  • ADHD: low dopamine & norepinephrine; inattention, hyperactivity, impulsiveness.
  • Increased ICP: skull holds brain + blood + CSF. Early = headache/N&V; Late = Cushing's Triad (↑BP, ↓HR, ↓RR), fixed pupils. Decorticate = flexed (to core); decerebrate = extended.
  • Headaches: tension (bilateral band), sinus (facial), cluster (one eye, stabbing), migraine (unilateral throbbing + photophobia).
  • Stroke: Ischemic (80%, clot, give fibrinolytics AFTER CT) vs Hemorrhagic (rupture, #1 = HTN, thunderclap HA). TIA = temporary, a warning. Bell's palsy = face only (incl. forehead), no limbs.
  • Brain bleeds: Epidural = arterial/fast (above dura); Subdural = venous/slow (below dura). Meningitis triad = stiff neck + headache + fever; encephalitis = brain tissue (confusion). Hydrocephalus: infant head enlarges, adult ↑ICP.
  • SCI: "C5 keeps diaphragm alive." Spinal shock = flaccid/no reflexes; Neurogenic shock = ↓BP + ↓HR (above T6). Autonomic dysreflexia = severe HTN + bradycardia from irritant below injury (full bladder). Status epilepticus = seizure >5 min. Spina bifida = folate deficiency.
🦴 MODULE 6 — MSK, Skin & Burns
  • Trauma: Primary survey = ABCDE. Fracture neurovascular check = 5 P's (Pain, Pulse, Pallor, Paresthesia, Paralysis). Curves: kyphosis (forward), lordosis (sway), scoliosis (sideways).
  • Bone: Osteoporosis = demineralization (menopause/steroids/low Ca). Osteomalacia = soft bone (vit D; rickets in kids). OA = wear & tear, worse through the day. Osteomyelitis = bone infection (open fracture → sepsis).
  • Fracture complications: Compartment syndrome (6 P's). Fat embolism triad = confusion + petechiae + dyspnea. Rhabdomyolysis = myalgia + brown urine + weakness (↑K⁺, ↑CK).
  • Soft tissue: Sprain = ligament; Strain = muscle/tendon. Rotator cuff = pain with abduction. ACL = plant-and-twist.
  • Skin infections: Impetigo (superficial, honey crust) vs Cellulitis (deep, dermis/subcutaneous). HSV-1 oral, HSV-2 genital. Measles = 3 C's + Koplik spots, rash head-to-toe. Fungi love warm/moist.
  • Chronic skin: Eczema (flexor surfaces, asthma/allergy), Urticaria (IgE/histamine hives), Acne (sebum + plug + bacteria). Cancers: Basal (common, rarely spreads) < Squamous < Melanoma (deadliest, ABCDE).
  • Ulcers: Pressure (ischemia over bony spot) vs Venous stasis (failing leg vein valves). Burns: 1st = red; 2nd = blisters; 3rd = painless/charred. Complications: hypovolemic shock, infection/sepsis, AKI, hypothermia.

📚 Glossary

Mental Health & Neuro Terms
  • Positive vs negative symptoms — added (psychosis) vs lost (the 3 A's) function in schizophrenia.
  • ICP — intracranial pressure (brain + blood + CSF in the skull).
  • Cushing's Triad — ↑BP, ↓HR, ↓RR; late sign of high ICP.
  • Decorticate / Decerebrate — flexed (to core) / extended posturing.
  • Uncal herniation — temporal lobe pushed through the tentorial notch, compressing the brainstem.
  • CVA / TIA — stroke (cell death) / transient ischemic attack (temporary, resolves).
  • Epidural / Subdural — arterial bleed above dura / venous bleed below dura.
  • Nuchal rigidity — stiff neck (meningitis).
  • Autonomic dysreflexia — SCI emergency: severe HTN + bradycardia from an irritant.
  • Status epilepticus — seizure >5 min or repeated without recovery.
MSK, Skin & Burn Terms
  • Primary survey (ABCDE) — trauma life-threat assessment.
  • 5 P's — Pain, Pulse, Pallor, Paresthesia, Paralysis (fracture neurovascular check).
  • Kyphosis / Lordosis / Scoliosis — forward / inward / sideways spinal curves.
  • Osteoporosis / Osteomalacia — demineralized / soft (vit D) bone.
  • Osteomyelitis — bone infection.
  • Compartment syndrome — high compartment pressure (6 P's).
  • Fat embolism — marrow fat in blood (confusion, petechiae, dyspnea).
  • Rhabdomyolysis — muscle breakdown (myoglobinuria, ↑K⁺, ↑CK).
  • Sprain / Strain — ligament / muscle-tendon injury.
  • Impetigo / Cellulitis — superficial honey-crust / deep dermal infection.
  • Urticaria — IgE/histamine hives. ABCDE — melanoma warning signs.
  • Decubitus / Venous stasis ulcer — pressure ulcer / failing-vein leg ulcer.

🎯 Final Study Tips

  • This exam is pattern recognition — a scenario describes a patho or symptom set, and you name the condition. Drill the one hallmark for each disorder.
  • Memorize the lettered/numbered sets: Cushing's Triad, ABCDE (trauma) & ABCDE (melanoma), the 5 P's and 6 P's, the fat embolism and rhabdo triads, the meningitis triad.
