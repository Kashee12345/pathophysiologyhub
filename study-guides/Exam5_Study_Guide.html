📚 Glossary
TOPIC 1 🩸 Anemias: Overview & Types MUST KNOW▼

Anemia = reduced oxygen-carrying capacity (low hemoglobin/RBCs). Whatever the cause, the general signs/symptoms are the same: fatigue, pallor, tachycardia, dyspnea on exertion, dizziness, cold intolerance — all from tissue hypoxia.

Classify by red-cell size (MCV) — the fastest first cut

TypePatho / causeHallmark clue
Iron-deficiency (microcytic)Too little iron → ↓ hemoglobin (blood loss, poor intake, pregnancy)↓ ferritin, spoon nails (koilonychia), pica
Thalassemia (microcytic)Inherited defect → reduced globin-chain production → abnormal/fragile RBCsNormal iron studies, target cells, family history
B12 / folate (macrocytic)Impaired DNA synthesis → large, immature RBCsB12 → neuro signs (paresthesias, ataxia); folate does not
Sickle cell (normo, hemolytic)Point mutation → HbS sickles when deoxygenated → vaso-occlusion + hemolysisPain crises; triggers: hypoxia, dehydration, infection, cold
Aplastic (normo)Bone-marrow failure → pancytopenia (drugs, radiation, idiopathic)All lines low → anemia + infection + bleeding; low reticulocytes
Hemolytic (normo)RBCs destroyed faster than made↑ reticulocytes, ↑ indirect bilirubin, ↑ LDH, ↓ haptoglobin; jaundice

B12 deficiency & pernicious anemia

Pernicious anemia is the classic B12 cause: an autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor (made by gastric parietal cells), so B12 can't be absorbed. Signs: macrocytic anemia + neurologic symptoms (numbness/tingling, balance problems), glossitis, and fatigue.

🧠 Memory hooks
Microcytic = "small & iron-poor" (iron deficiency, thalassemia). Macrocytic = "big & DNA-broken" (B12/folate). Only B12 gives neuro signs.   Aplastic = "absent marrow" → everything low.   Pernicious = "no intrinsic factor."
⚠ Common confusion
Iron deficiency vs thalassemia (both microcytic): iron deficiency has low ferritin; thalassemia has normal iron studies and a family history. B12 vs folate (both macrocytic): only B12 causes neurologic symptoms.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. A patient has microcytic anemia with low ferritin, brittle spoon-shaped nails, and cravings for ice. Which anemia?
    Show answer
    Iron-deficiency anemia. Low ferritin (empty stores), koilonychia (spoon nails), and pica are classic.
  2. A vegan patient develops macrocytic anemia plus numbness and an unsteady gait. B12 or folate deficiency, and why?
    Show answer
    B12 deficiency — only B12 (needed for myelin) causes the neurologic signs. Folate deficiency gives the same big cells without neuro involvement.
  3. SATA: Which are general signs/symptoms of anemia? (a) fatigue, (b) pallor, (c) tachycardia, (d) hypertension crisis, (e) dyspnea on exertion.
    Show answer
    a, b, c, e. Anemia causes fatigue, pallor, tachycardia (compensatory), and exertional dyspnea. A hypertensive crisis is not an anemia feature.
  4. A patient has pancytopenia (low RBCs, WBCs, and platelets) with a low reticulocyte count after chemotherapy. Which anemia?
    Show answer
    Aplastic anemia — bone-marrow failure drops all three cell lines; the marrow can't respond, so reticulocytes are low.
🩺 Clinical scenario
A child with sickle cell disease is admitted with severe bone pain after playing outside in cold weather while dehydrated. What is happening and why?

A vaso-occlusive crisis: cold, dehydration, and hypoxia caused HbS to polymerize/sickle, and the rigid cells block small vessels → ischemia and pain. Management targets the triggers — hydration, oxygen, warmth, and pain control.
TOPIC 2 🧿 Bleeding, Clotting & Transfusion MUST KNOW▼

Module 9's other half: too little clotting (bleeding disorders), too much/consumed clotting (DIC), too many cells (polycythemia vera), and the dangerous transfusion reaction.

DisorderPatho
Von Willebrand diseaseDeficient/defective von Willebrand factor → platelets can't adhere well → mucosal bleeding (nosebleeds, easy bruising, heavy menses), prolonged bleeding time. Most common inherited bleeding disorder.
Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)Widespread clotting consumes platelets and clotting factors → then severe bleeding. Triggered by sepsis, trauma, obstetric events, malignancy. Labs: ↑PT, ↑aPTT, ↓platelets, ↓fibrinogen, ↑↑D-dimer.
Polycythemia veraJAK2 mutation → marrow overproduces RBCs (± WBCs/platelets) → thick, viscous blood. S/S: ruddy (red) complexion, headache, pruritus after a warm shower, hypertension, thrombosis risk.
Acute hemolytic transfusion reactionABO incompatibility → recipient antibodies attack donor RBCs → rapid intravascular hemolysis within minutes.

Acute hemolytic reaction — why it happens & what you see

If mismatched (ABO-incompatible) blood is given, the recipient's preformed antibodies bind donor red cells and destroy them. You see fever, chills, flank/back pain, dark urine (hemoglobinuria), hypotension within minutes — it can progress to shock, DIC, and acute kidney injury. First action: STOP the transfusion and keep the line open with saline.

🧠 Memory hooks
vWF = "the platelet glue" (its loss = mucosal bleeding). DIC = "clots then bleeds" (everything consumed; D-dimer sky-high). Polycythemia vera = "too much, too thick, too red" → clot risk. Acute hemolytic reaction = ABO mismatch → STOP + saline.
⚠ Common confusion
Polycythemia vera is too many cells (thrombosis risk); the anemias are too few. And in DIC the paradox is clotting and bleeding at once — not one or the other.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. Minutes into a transfusion, a patient develops fever, chills, flank pain, and dark urine. What is happening and what is the priority action?
    Show answer
    An acute hemolytic transfusion reaction (ABO incompatibility) — recipient antibodies destroy donor RBCs. Stop the transfusion immediately and keep the line open with normal saline; notify the provider/blood bank.
  2. A patient with sepsis is bleeding from IV sites with ↑PT, ↑aPTT, low platelets, low fibrinogen, and very high D-dimer. Which disorder?
    Show answer
    DIC. Widespread clotting consumes platelets and factors (so PT/aPTT rise, platelets/fibrinogen fall) while fibrin breakdown drives the very high D-dimer — the patient clots and bleeds at once.
  3. A patient has a ruddy complexion, headaches, itching after warm showers, and a JAK2 mutation. Which disorder, and what is the main risk?
    Show answer
    Polycythemia vera. The thickened, hyperviscous blood raises the risk of thrombosis (clots) — treated with phlebotomy and low-dose aspirin.
🩺 Clinical scenario
A teenager reports lifelong easy bruising, frequent nosebleeds, and heavy periods, with a normal platelet count but a prolonged bleeding time. What is the likely disorder and mechanism?

Von Willebrand disease — deficient/defective vWF impairs platelet adhesion (and destabilizes factor VIII), producing mucosal-type bleeding with a normal platelet count but prolonged bleeding time.
TOPIC 3 🥬 Atherosclerosis & Coronary Artery Disease MUST KNOW▼

Atherosclerosis is the buildup of lipid plaque in artery walls — the root of coronary artery disease (CAD), MI, stroke, and peripheral artery disease.

Stages of advancement

  1. Endothelial injury (from hypertension, smoking, high LDL, diabetes) — the starting point.
  2. Fatty streak — LDL enters the wall, is oxidized, and macrophages engulf it → foam cells.
  3. Fibrous plaque — smooth muscle and collagen form a cap over the lipid core; the lumen narrows.
  4. Complicated (unstable) plaque — the cap ruptures → a clot (thrombus) forms → acute occlusion (MI/stroke).

Risk factors

ModifiableNon-modifiable
Smoking, hypertension, high LDL/low HDL, diabetes, obesity, sedentary lifestyle, poor diet, stressAge, male sex, family history/genetics
🧠 Memory hook
Progression: Injury → Fatty streak (foam cells) → Fibrous plaque → Rupture & clot. The rupture is what turns stable disease into an acute MI — a stable narrowing suddenly becomes a total blockage.
⚠ Common confusion
A stable plaque narrows the lumen (causes stable angina with exertion). An unstable plaque that ruptures triggers a clot and causes acute coronary syndrome (unstable angina/MI) — the danger is rupture, not just size.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. Which event most directly converts stable atherosclerotic disease into an acute myocardial infarction?
    Show answer
    Plaque rupture with thrombus (clot) formation — the exposed lipid core triggers a clot that suddenly occludes the artery.
  2. Which cells form the fatty streak, the earliest visible atherosclerotic lesion?
    Show answer
    Foam cells — macrophages that have engulfed oxidized LDL within the injured vessel wall.
  3. SATA: Which are modifiable risk factors for CAD? (a) smoking, (b) hypertension, (c) family history, (d) diabetes, (e) age.
    Show answer
    a, b, d. Smoking, hypertension, and diabetes are modifiable. Family history (c) and age (e) are non-modifiable.
🩺 Clinical scenario
A 58-year-old smoker with hypertension and diabetes has chest tightness when climbing stairs that resolves with rest. What stage/type of disease does this suggest?

A stable fibrous plaque narrowing a coronary artery → stable angina: demand-related chest pain with exertion that resolves with rest. His modifiable risk factors (smoking, HTN, diabetes) are driving the atherosclerosis.
TOPIC 4 💓 Angina, MI & Troponin MUST KNOW▼

When myocardial oxygen demand exceeds supply, the heart becomes ischemic. The spectrum runs from angina to full infarction.

ConditionPathoKey features
Stable anginaFixed narrowing; ischemia only when demand rises (exertion)Predictable pain, relieved by rest/nitroglycerin; no cell death
Unstable anginaPlaque rupture/partial clot; ischemia at restNew/worsening pain at rest; normal troponin (no infarct yet) — a warning sign
NSTEMIPartial coronary occlusion → subendocardial infarct↑ Troponin, ST depression/T-wave changes (no ST elevation)
STEMIComplete coronary occlusion → full-thickness infarct↑ Troponin + ST elevation — a true emergency (reperfuse fast)

Troponin & MI symptoms

Troponin is a protein released when heart muscle is damaged, so it is elevated in MI (NSTEMI/STEMI) and other myocardial injury (e.g., myocarditis). It is the key lab to distinguish unstable angina (normal troponin) from an infarct (elevated troponin). Classic MI symptoms: crushing substernal chest pain (may radiate to left arm/jaw), diaphoresis, nausea, dyspnea; women/elderly/diabetics may have atypical presentations.

Sudden cardiac death

The most common cause of sudden cardiac death is a lethal ventricular arrhythmia (ventricular fibrillation), usually triggered by underlying coronary artery disease/ischemia.

🧠 Memory hooks
Stable = with exertion, relieved by rest. Unstable = at rest, troponin normal. NSTEMI = troponin up, ST down/partial. STEMI = troponin up + ST elevation, total occlusion.   "STEMI = ST-Elevation = Emergency."
⚠ Common confusion
The line between unstable angina and NSTEMI is troponin: both can have rest pain and no ST elevation, but only NSTEMI has positive troponin (actual cell death).

❓ Practice Questions

  1. A patient has chest pain at rest, no ST elevation, and a normal troponin. Which diagnosis?
    Show answer
    Unstable angina — rest pain from a ruptured/partially occluding plaque, but troponin is normal because no infarction has occurred yet. It's a warning that an MI may follow.
  2. A patient has crushing chest pain, ST elevation on ECG, and elevated troponin. Which diagnosis and how urgent?
    Show answer
    STEMI — a complete coronary occlusion causing full-thickness infarction. It's a time-critical emergency requiring immediate reperfusion (PCI or thrombolytics).
  3. When is troponin elevated?
    Show answer
    When there is myocardial cell damage — classically MI (NSTEMI/STEMI), and also in myocarditis or other cardiac injury. It stays normal in stable and unstable angina (no infarct).
🩺 Clinical scenario
A 62-year-old collapses; monitor shows ventricular fibrillation. What is the most common underlying cause of sudden cardiac death here?

Coronary artery disease/ischemia triggering a lethal ventricular arrhythmia (VF). Immediate defibrillation and CPR are the priority.
TOPIC 5 🏯 Heart Failure & Preload/Afterload MUST KNOW▼

Heart failure = the heart can't pump enough to meet the body's needs. Split it two ways: which side and systolic vs diastolic.

Systolic HF (HFrEF)Diastolic HF (HFpEF)
ProblemWeak pump — ↓ contractility → ↓ ejection fraction (<40%)Stiff ventricle — can't relax/fill; ejection fraction preserved (>50%)
CausesMI, dilated cardiomyopathy, chronic ischemia, valve regurgitationLong-standing hypertension with LVH, hypertrophic/restrictive cardiomyopathy, aging
Left-sided HFRight-sided HF
Backs up intothe lungsthe systemic veins/body
SignsDyspnea, orthopnea, crackles, pink frothy sputum, pulmonary edemaJVD, peripheral edema, hepatomegaly, ascites, weight gain
Common causeHTN, ischemic/valve diseaseMost often left HF; also lung disease (cor pulmonale)

Preload, afterload & the natriuretic peptides

  • Preload = the volume/stretch in the ventricle before it contracts. ↑ by fluid overload, valve regurgitation; ↓ by dehydration/hemorrhage.
  • Afterload = the resistance the ventricle pumps against. ↑ by hypertension and aortic stenosis (the heart works harder).
  • ANP/BNP are released by stretched heart chambers (volume overload). They promote sodium and water excretion + vasodilation to lower volume/pressure — the body's own diuretic. BNP is elevated in heart failure and used to diagnose it.
🧠 Memory hooks
Left = Lungs (pulmonary congestion). Right = Rest of the body (JVD, edema). Systolic = squeeze problem (↓EF); diastolic = fill problem (stiff, EF preserved). BNP up = the heart is stretched/failing.
⚠ Common confusion
Preload vs afterload: preload = volume coming in (stretch before contraction); afterload = resistance pumping out (raised by hypertension). And the #1 cause of right HF is left HF.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. A patient has dyspnea, orthopnea, crackles, and pink frothy sputum. Left or right heart failure, and why?
    Show answer
    Left-sided HF — blood backs up into the lungs (pulmonary congestion) → dyspnea, crackles, and pulmonary edema.
  2. A patient has jugular venous distension, peripheral edema, and hepatomegaly. Which side of the heart is failing?
    Show answer
    Right-sided HF — blood backs up into the systemic veins → JVD, dependent edema, and liver congestion.
  3. Which condition most increases afterload on the left ventricle?
    Show answer
    Hypertension (and aortic stenosis) — the LV must generate more force to eject against the higher resistance.
  4. Why is BNP elevated in heart failure?
    Show answer
    Volume overload stretches the heart chambers, which release BNP to promote sodium/water excretion and vasodilation. A high BNP supports a heart-failure diagnosis.
🩺 Clinical scenario
A patient with long-standing hypertension has exertional dyspnea; echo shows a preserved ejection fraction but a thick, stiff left ventricle. Which type of heart failure?

Diastolic heart failure (HFpEF) — chronic hypertension caused LV hypertrophy; the stiff ventricle can't relax and fill well, even though the squeeze (EF) is preserved.
TOPIC 6 🩸 Hypertension & Blood-Loss Compensation MUST KNOW▼

Hypertension is chronically elevated arterial pressure. It's usually "silent" (few symptoms) until it damages organs — which is why it's the "silent killer."

Contributing factors & symptoms

  • Contributing factors: age, obesity, high sodium intake, sedentary lifestyle, excess alcohol, smoking, stress, family history, diabetes, kidney disease (most primary/essential HTN has no single cause).
  • Common symptoms: often none; when present — headache, dizziness, blurred vision; severe elevations can cause chest pain or epistaxis.

Complications (target-organ damage)

OrganDamage
HeartLV hypertrophy → heart failure; accelerates CAD/MI
BrainStroke, aneurysm
KidneysChronic kidney disease (damages small renal vessels)
Eyes / vesselsRetinopathy; aneurysm/dissection; peripheral artery disease

Compensatory mechanisms during blood loss

When blood volume drops (hemorrhage), the body defends blood pressure in a set order:

  • Fast (seconds): baroreceptors trigger sympathetic activation → tachycardia, increased contractility, vasoconstriction.
  • Minutes–hours: RAAS (renin → angiotensin II vasoconstriction + aldosterone Na/water retention) and ADH (water retention) restore volume.
  • Slow (days): the kidney releases erythropoietin → more RBCs; fluid shifts from interstitium into vessels.
🧠 Memory hooks
HTN = the "silent killer" that damages the heart, brain, kidneys, and eyes. Blood-loss compensation order: Sympathetic (fast) → RAAS/ADH (volume) → EPO (slow).
⚠ Common confusion
Hypertension is usually asymptomatic — don't wait for symptoms. The danger is silent target-organ damage (stroke, HF, CKD) over years.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. SATA: Which are complications of chronic hypertension? (a) stroke, (b) heart failure/LVH, (c) chronic kidney disease, (d) improved vision, (e) MI.
    Show answer
    a, b, c, e. HTN damages the brain (stroke), heart (LVH/HF, MI), and kidneys (CKD). It harms, not improves, vision (retinopathy).
  2. Which is the FIRST compensatory response to acute blood loss?
    Show answer
    Baroreceptor-mediated sympathetic activation — immediate tachycardia, increased contractility, and vasoconstriction to defend blood pressure (RAAS/ADH and EPO follow more slowly).
  3. Why is hypertension called the "silent killer"?
    Show answer
    It's usually asymptomatic while quietly causing target-organ damage (heart, brain, kidney, eyes) over time.
🩺 Clinical scenario
A trauma patient loses a large volume of blood and becomes tachycardic with cool, pale skin and a narrow pulse pressure. What compensation explains these findings?

Rapid sympathetic activation from baroreceptors: tachycardia (to maintain output) and peripheral vasoconstriction (cool, pale skin) defend blood pressure while RAAS/ADH work to restore volume.
TOPIC 7 ⚡ Arrhythmias & Atrial Fibrillation MUST KNOW▼

An arrhythmia is an abnormal heart rhythm from disordered electrical activity. The exam favorite is atrial fibrillation.

Atrial fibrillation (AFib)

  • Patho: chaotic, disorganized atrial electrical activity → the atria quiver instead of contracting → irregularly irregular rhythm, loss of the coordinated "atrial kick."
  • Biggest complication: blood pools/stagnates in the fibrillating atria → clots form → a clot can embolize → stroke (which is why AFib patients are anticoagulated). Can also cause ↓ cardiac output and heart failure.

Arrhythmia complications in general

Depending on rate/rhythm: decreased cardiac output (dizziness, syncope, hypotension), thromboembolism/stroke (AFib), heart failure, and — with lethal ventricular rhythms (VT/VF) — sudden cardiac death.

🧠 Memory hook
AFib = "quivering atria → pooled blood → clot → stroke." That embolic-stroke risk is why AFib patients get anticoagulation. "Irregularly irregular" pulse = AFib.
⚠ Common confusion
Atrial fibrillation (AFib) → stroke risk, often chronic/manageable. Ventricular fibrillation (VF) → no effective pumping → cardiac arrest requiring immediate defibrillation. Don't mix the two.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. Which mechanism explains the increased stroke risk in atrial fibrillation?
    Show answer
    The atria quiver instead of contracting, so blood stagnates and clots; a clot can embolize to the brain → stroke. This is why AFib patients are anticoagulated.
  2. SATA: Which are potential complications of arrhythmias? (a) decreased cardiac output, (b) thromboembolic stroke, (c) heart failure, (d) sudden cardiac death, (e) improved perfusion.
    Show answer
    a, b, c, d. Arrhythmias can drop cardiac output, throw clots (AFib), precipitate HF, and cause sudden death (VT/VF). They don't improve perfusion.
🩺 Clinical scenario
An older adult with a new "irregularly irregular" pulse suddenly develops right-sided weakness and slurred speech. What likely happened?

New-onset atrial fibrillation allowed a clot to form in the stagnant atrium; it embolized to the brain → an embolic stroke. This is exactly why AFib warrants anticoagulation.
TOPIC 8 🩵 Inflammatory & Valvular Heart Disease MUST KNOW▼

Infections and inflammation can strike each layer of the heart, and the valves. Match the layer to the disease.

ConditionPatho / signs
PericarditisInflammation of the pericardium → sharp, pleuritic chest pain relieved by sitting forward, a friction rub
Pericardial effusionFluid accumulates in the pericardial sac; may cause muffled heart sounds, dyspnea
Cardiac tamponadeRapid/large effusion compresses the heart → can't fill → ↓ cardiac output. Beck's triad: hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds — emergency (pericardiocentesis)
MyocarditisInflammation of the myocardium (often viral) → weakened pump, arrhythmias, HF; may ↑ troponin
Infective endocarditisInfection of the endocardium/valves → vegetations. Fever, new murmur, Janeway lesions, Osler nodes, splinter hemorrhages; risk with IV drug use, prosthetic valves
Rheumatic heart diseaseAutoimmune reaction after untreated strep (rheumatic fever) → scars heart valves (esp. mitral) over time

Valvular disorders (mitral/aortic)

  • Stenosis = valve too narrow/stiff → the chamber behind it works harder to push blood through → pressure overload/hypertrophy.
  • Regurgitation (insufficiency) = valve leaks backward → blood flows the wrong way → volume overload.
  • Net effect: reduced forward output, murmurs, and eventually heart failure.
🧠 Memory hooks
Layers: Pericarditis (sac, pain better leaning forward), Myocarditis (muscle, weak pump), Endocarditis (valves, vegetations). Tamponade = Beck's triad (hypotension + JVD + muffled sounds). Rheumatic = after strep → mitral valve. Stenosis = stiff/narrow; regurg = leaky.
⚠ Common confusion
Pericardial effusion vs tamponade: an effusion is just fluid; tamponade is when that fluid compresses the heart and drops cardiac output — an emergency. Pericarditis pain improves leaning forward (vs MI pain).

❓ Practice Questions

  1. A patient has hypotension, JVD, and muffled heart sounds after a large pericardial effusion. What is this triad and diagnosis?
    Show answer
    Beck's triad → cardiac tamponade. Fluid compresses the heart so it can't fill; cardiac output falls — an emergency treated with pericardiocentesis.
  2. A patient has sharp chest pain relieved by leaning forward and a pericardial friction rub. Which condition?
    Show answer
    Pericarditis. The positional pain (better sitting forward) and friction rub are classic — and help distinguish it from MI.
  3. Untreated streptococcal pharyngitis can lead to which chronic valvular disease, and which valve is most affected?
    Show answer
    Rheumatic heart disease — an autoimmune reaction after strep scars the valves, most commonly the mitral valve.
🩺 Clinical scenario
A person who injects drugs develops fever, a new heart murmur, and small painful nodules on the fingers with splinter hemorrhages under the nails. What is the likely diagnosis and mechanism?

Infective endocarditis — bacteria seed the valves and form vegetations; pieces embolize peripherally (Osler nodes, Janeway lesions, splinter hemorrhages). IV drug use is a major risk factor.
TOPIC 9 🦵 Peripheral Vascular Disease HIGH YIELD▼

Vascular disease outside the heart: arteries (poor inflow), veins (poor outflow), and vasospasm.

ConditionPatho & signs
Peripheral artery disease (PAD)Atherosclerosis narrows leg arteries → poor arterial inflow. Signs: intermittent claudication, cool/pale/hairless legs, weak pulses, pain worse with elevation, wounds that don't heal (arterial ulcers on toes/feet)
Intermittent claudicationThe hallmark PAD symptom: cramping leg/calf pain with walking that is relieved by rest (muscle ischemia when demand rises)
Venous insufficiencyIncompetent valves → poor venous return → blood pools. Signs: edema, brown/hyperpigmented skin, venous stasis ulcers (medial ankle); complications include chronic ulcers and DVT risk
Raynaud'sVasospasm of small arteries (triggered by cold/stress) → fingers/toes turn white → blue → red with numbness, then rewarming
🧠 Memory hooks
PAD = arterial = pain with walking (claudication), cool/pale, distal ulcers. Venous = pooling = edema, brown skin, ankle ulcers. Raynaud's = white-blue-red with cold/stress.
⚠ Common confusion
Arterial (PAD) vs venous: arterial = poor inflow (cool, pale, pain with activity, distal/toe ulcers); venous = poor outflow (warm, edematous, brown skin, ankle ulcers). Opposite pictures.

❓ Practice Questions

  1. A patient reports cramping calf pain when walking that stops after resting a few minutes. Which sign, and which disease?
    Show answer
    Intermittent claudication → peripheral artery disease. Narrowed arteries can't meet the higher oxygen demand of walking, so the calf becomes ischemic until rest lowers demand.
  2. A patient's fingers turn white, then blue, then red with numbness when exposed to cold. Which condition?
    Show answer
    Raynaud's — cold/stress-triggered vasospasm of the digital arteries produces the classic white→blue→red color change.
  3. SATA: Which suggest chronic venous insufficiency? (a) lower-leg edema, (b) brown/hyperpigmented skin, (c) ulcers at the medial ankle, (d) cool pale foot with absent pulses, (e) pain with walking relieved by rest.
    Show answer
    a, b, c. Edema, brown skin, and medial-ankle ulcers are venous. (d) and (e) are arterial (PAD) findings.
🩺 Clinical scenario
A diabetic smoker has a cool, pale, hairless lower leg with weak pulses and a non-healing ulcer on the toe. Arterial or venous, and why?

Arterial (PAD) — atherosclerosis limits inflow, so the tissue is cool, pale, and hairless with weak pulses and poor healing; ulcers form distally (toes/feet). Smoking and diabetes are major risk factors.
TOPIC 10 👶 Congenital Heart Defects HIGH YIELD▼

Congenital heart defects are structural problems present at birth. The key split is cyanotic vs non-cyanotic — based on whether deoxygenated blood reaches the systemic circulation.

Non-cyanotic (acyanotic)Cyanotic
ShuntLeft → right (oxygenated blood recirculates through the lungs)Right → left (deoxygenated blood enters the body)
Cyanosis?No (blood is still oxygenated)Yes (bluish skin)
ExamplesVSD, ASD, PDA, coarctation of the aortaTetralogy of Fallot, transposition of the great arteries

Tetralogy of Fallot (the classic cyanotic defect)

Four defects (mnemonic PROVe): Pulmonary stenosis, Right ventricular hypertrophy, Overriding aorta, Ventricular septal defect. Because pulmonary stenosis + the VSD push deoxygenated blood out to the body, the child is cyanotic. Signs: cyanosis, "tet spells" (worsening cyanosis with crying/feeding), and a child who may squat to relieve a spell.

🧠 Memory hooks
Left→Right = no cyanosis (oxygenated blood recirculates): VSD, ASD, PDA. Right→Left = cyanosis (deoxygenated blood to body): Tetralogy of Fallot, transposition. ToF = PROVe (Pulmonary stenosis, RVH, Overriding aorta, VSD).
⚠ Common confusion
Direction of the shunt sets the color: L→R shunts do NOT cause cyanosis (blood re-runs through the lungs), while R→L shunts cause cyanosis (deoxygenated blood bypasses the lungs into the body).

❓ Practice Questions

  1. Which type of shunt causes cyanosis, and give one example defect.
    Show answer
    A right-to-left shunt causes cyanosis because deoxygenated blood enters the systemic circulation. Example: Tetralogy of Fallot (or transposition of the great arteries).
  2. What are the four components of Tetralogy of Fallot?
    Show answer
    PROVe: Pulmonary stenosis, Right ventricular hypertrophy, Overriding aorta, Ventricular septal defect.
  3. SATA: Which are non-cyanotic (left-to-right) defects? (a) VSD, (b) ASD, (c) Tetralogy of Fallot, (d) PDA, (e) transposition of the great arteries.
    Show answer
    a, b, d. VSD, ASD, and PDA are non-cyanotic (left-to-right). Tetralogy of Fallot and transposition are cyanotic (right-to-left).
🩺 Clinical scenario
An infant becomes deeply cyanotic during crying and feeding and instinctively squats. What defect, and why does squatting help?

Tetralogy of Fallot ("tet spells"). Squatting raises systemic vascular resistance, which reduces the right-to-left shunt and pushes more blood through the lungs — temporarily improving oxygenation.

🎯 Exam Cheat Sheet — Night-Before Review

Module 9 — Blood Disorders

  • Anemia s/s (all types): fatigue, pallor, tachycardia, dyspnea on exertion, dizziness.
  • Iron deficiency = microcytic, ↓ferritin, spoon nails, pica. Thalassemia = microcytic, normal iron, inherited, target cells.
  • B12/folate = macrocytic (DNA defect); only B12 → neuro signs. Pernicious anemia = autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor → no B12 absorption.
  • Sickle cell = HbS sickles when deoxygenated → vaso-occlusion (triggers: hypoxia, dehydration, cold, infection). Aplastic = marrow failure → pancytopenia.
  • Von Willebrand = ↓vWF → mucosal bleeding, normal platelets, long bleeding time. DIC = clots then bleeds; ↑PT/aPTT, ↓platelets/fibrinogen, ↑↑D-dimer.
  • Polycythemia vera = JAK2, too many RBCs, ruddy, itchy after warm shower, clot risk. Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction = ABO mismatch → STOP + saline.

Module 10 — Cardiovascular

  • Atherosclerosis: injury → fatty streak (foam cells) → fibrous plaque → rupture + clot = acute MI. CAD modifiable risks: smoking, HTN, high LDL, diabetes.
  • Angina/MI: stable = exertion, relieved by rest. Unstable = rest, troponin normal. NSTEMI = ↑troponin, no ST elevation. STEMI = ↑troponin + ST elevation (emergency). Troponin = cardiac injury marker. Sudden cardiac death = VF from CAD.
  • Heart failure: Left = Lungs (dyspnea, crackles); Right = Rest of body (JVD, edema). Systolic = weak squeeze (↓EF); diastolic = stiff, can't fill (EF preserved). #1 cause of right HF = left HF. BNP up = HF.
  • Preload = filling volume/stretch; afterload = resistance to eject (↑ by HTN). ANP/BNP = released when heart is stretched → excrete Na/water, vasodilate.
  • Hypertension = silent; damages heart, brain (stroke), kidney, eyes. Blood-loss compensation: sympathetic (fast) → RAAS/ADH → EPO (slow).
  • AFib = quivering atria → clot → stroke (anticoagulate). VFib = cardiac arrest → defibrillate.
  • Pericarditis = pain better leaning forward + rub. Tamponade = Beck's triad (hypotension, JVD, muffled sounds). Myocarditis = viral, weak pump. Endocarditis = valve vegetations (IV drug use). Rheumatic = post-strep → mitral valve.
  • PAD = arterial, claudication, cool/pale, toe ulcers. Venous insufficiency = edema, brown skin, ankle ulcers. Raynaud's = white-blue-red with cold.
  • Congenital: L→R = no cyanosis (VSD, ASD, PDA); R→L = cyanotic (Tetralogy of Fallot = PROVe, transposition).

📚 Glossary

Anemia — reduced oxygen-carrying capacity (low Hb/RBCs).

MCV — average red-cell size; classifies anemia as micro-, normo-, or macrocytic.

Ferritin — stored iron; low in iron-deficiency anemia.

Intrinsic factor — gastric protein needed to absorb B12; lost in pernicious anemia.

von Willebrand factor — protein that helps platelets adhere; deficient in vWD.

DIC — disseminated intravascular coagulation: widespread clotting then bleeding.

Polycythemia vera — JAK2-driven overproduction of RBCs → thick blood, clot risk.

Atherosclerosis — lipid plaque buildup in artery walls.

Troponin — protein released with myocardial injury; elevated in MI/myocarditis.

STEMI / NSTEMI — complete vs partial coronary occlusion causing infarction.

Ejection fraction — % of blood ejected per beat; reduced in systolic HF.

Preload / Afterload — ventricular filling volume / resistance to ejection.

BNP — peptide released by stretched heart; elevated in heart failure.

Atrial fibrillation — disorganized atrial activity → clot/stroke risk.

Cardiac tamponade — pericardial fluid compressing the heart (Beck's triad).

Intermittent claudication — exertional leg pain relieved by rest (PAD).

Tetralogy of Fallot — cyanotic defect: pulmonary stenosis, RVH, overriding aorta, VSD.

🎯 Final Study Tips

  • Blood: anchor every anemia to its lab clue — MCV size (micro/normo/macro), ferritin, and the one hallmark (spoon nails, neuro signs, sickled cells).
  • Heart: cause → effect. Ask "forward or backward failure?", "supply or demand ischemia?", "left or right?" and the signs follow.
  • Lock in the opposite pairs: stable vs unstable angina, NSTEMI vs STEMI, systolic vs diastolic HF, left vs right HF, cyanotic vs non-cyanotic defects, preload vs afterload.
  • Know the emergencies: STEMI, cardiac tamponade (Beck's triad), DIC, acute hemolytic transfusion reaction.
  • Troponin = the cardiac-injury marker (rises in MI/myocarditis). BNP = the heart-failure/volume marker.
  • Practice SATA carefully — read each option as its own true/false statement.
